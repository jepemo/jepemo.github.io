Nietzsche and Value Creation

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to...

  • Trace an inherited value to its incentives, social setting, psychological appeal, and practical effects.

  • Distinguish a value audit from both automatic obedience and arbitrary personal preference.

  • Draft one revised commitment that can be tested through action, cost, and accountability.

Idea in one sentence: A value is not above examination just because it feels familiar; but examining its history is only the beginning of the harder work of shaping a life that can answer for what it values.

Core Insight

Mina works on an accessibility team. Her organization celebrates public launches: the project with a new logo, a keynote slide, and a visible deadline gets praise. Quiet maintenance work rarely appears in a review.

After a difficult month, Mina starts repeating a rule to herself:

Work that no one notices is wasted.

The rule changes her choices. She postpones an unglamorous repair that would make the product easier to use for people with low vision. Instead, she spends the week on a feature that will look good in a presentation.

Mina did not invent the rule from nothing. It arrived through promotion criteria, status signals, anxiety about being overlooked, and a real desire to make good work visible. The rule may contain something worth preserving: public recognition can bring resources, trust, and influence. But it may also train Mina to treat visibility as the only measure of worth.

Nietzsche's question is not merely, “Do you agree with this rule?” It is more disruptive:

Where did this value come from, what does it reward, what does it make possible, and what does it make you become?

That inquiry is a genealogy of value. It turns a rule that feels natural into something with a history, incentives, effects, and alternatives.

The Initial Model: A Familiar Value Must Be True

We often inherit standards before we can name them:

  • a successful person is always available;
  • a good child never disappoints a parent;
  • a serious worker chooses status over rest;
  • a kind person never imposes a cost on anyone;
  • visible achievement proves that a life matters.

Some inherited values deserve preservation. Some are mixed. Some reward a narrow form of life while disguising their costs. The problem is not inheritance itself. No one begins with a blank value system.

The initial model is:

this value is widely praised
        ->
it must be universal and self-justifying
        ->
I should obey it whenever it applies

This model is attractive because it saves work. A ready-made value gives Mina a quick answer during uncertainty: choose the visible launch.

It breaks when the rule conflicts with something else she also cares about. The launch can bring recognition, while the repair may determine whether people can use the product at all. “Visibility proves worth” does not settle that conflict. It only makes one side of it loud.

Genealogy: Make the Value Inspectable

Plain meaning:

A genealogy asks how a value became authoritative and what it does in practice.

In Mina's situation:

She asks why visible launches receive praise, whose incentives they serve, what feelings the rule recruits, and what work becomes invisible when she accepts it.

Technical name:

Nietzsche uses genealogy to investigate moral ideas through their historical and psychological formation. He often treats apparently noble values with suspicion, asking whether they express fear, resentment, social control, a need for recognition, or some other force.

This is not a magic debunking machine. Discovering that a value has an origin does not prove it is false or worthless. Nearly every human value has an origin. A genealogy earns its keep when it reveals a conflict, cost, or hidden function that the familiar slogan concealed.

A Value Genealogy Card

Mina turns “work that no one notices is wasted” into a card she can inspect.

Question Mina's first answer What it reveals
What is the value claim? Visibility proves worth. The rule turns public notice into a measure of value.
Where did it gain force? Performance reviews, launch culture, praise for fast teams, and past experiences of being overlooked. The rule has institutional and personal sources, not timeless authority.
What does it promise? Recognition, career safety, influence, and proof that effort counted. The value meets real needs; dismissing it would be too easy.
What does it reward? Announcements, visible features, speed, and work that can be narrated. Some useful work becomes easier to see.
What does it hide or punish? Maintenance, accessibility, repair, rest, collaboration, and work whose beneficiaries are not influential. The rule makes a particular kind of contribution look like the whole picture.
What happens if I obey it for a year? Mina may build a portfolio and also leave a less usable product behind. Values are patterns of action, not decorations on a profile.

The card does not tell Mina that visibility is bad. It makes a previously automatic ranking available for judgment.

Value Creation Is Not “Anything I Like”

Nietzsche is famous for talking about the creation of values, but that phrase is easy to flatten into “make up your own rules.” His writing does not support such an easy version.

First, values are not ordinary momentary preferences. Mina may prefer tea today and coffee tomorrow. A value guides repeated choices, ranks competing goods, and shapes what costs she will accept. It has to survive contact with time, relationships, bodies, institutions, and consequences.

Second, creation is constrained. Mina cannot make a value real merely by declaring it. If she says, “I value accessibility,” but repeatedly chooses presentations over repairs, the declaration has little practical force. A value becomes part of a life through attention, habits, refusals, projects, and revisions.

Third, values can conflict. Nietzsche does not offer one clean formula that removes every collision. Truthfulness can conflict with comfort; independence can conflict with loyalty; public visibility can conflict with care for work that remains unseen. A mature value practice names the conflict instead of pretending that one word solves it.

This is... Example Why it matters
A preference “I would rather present than write documentation.” It may matter, but it does not yet guide a whole pattern of commitment.
An inherited value “The most visible work is the most valuable work.” It may be useful or harmful; its source and effect need inspection.
A revised commitment “I will treat accessibility repair as first-class work and make its benefits visible without pretending only visible work counts.” It guides action, accepts a cost, and can be tested over time.

A Worked Value Revision

Follow Mina from borrowed standard to a constrained act of self-authorship.

Starting point: a status rule

Mina has two tasks for the week:

  • build a presentation feature that could impress senior leaders;
  • repair keyboard navigation so people can complete a form without a mouse.

The organization will notice the first task more easily. Mina's inherited rule says the choice is obvious.

Step 1: Separate the rule from the facts

Facts:

  • the feature has a public deadline;
  • the navigation issue blocks some users;
  • Mina has limited time;
  • leaders reward visible launches.

Inherited value:

If my work is not publicly praised, it did not count.

The distinction matters because a deadline is a constraint, while the ranking of public praise over access is a value judgment.

Step 2: Ask what the value is doing

Mina notices that the rule protects her from a fear: quiet work may not lead to recognition or security. It also aligns with an institution that can measure launches more easily than long-term accessibility.

This is not an accusation. A genealogy should not pretend to read a person's hidden motives with certainty. It is a hypothesis Mina can test against patterns in her own choices and the organization's incentives.

Step 3: Name the cost of obedience

If Mina always follows the visibility rule, she may become skilled at gaining attention while letting users bear the cost of neglected maintenance. The rule may also narrow her own sense of achievement until only applause feels real.

Step 4: Create a revised commitment

Mina does not replace the rule with “visibility is evil.” She writes:

I will pursue work that improves access and make its effects visible to the people who allocate resources. I will not treat applause as the only evidence that my work has value.

The sentence holds two values together: accessibility and the practical need to communicate its importance.

Step 5: Give the value a form

Mina spends the morning fixing the keyboard path, records the before-and-after experience, and adds a short accessibility impact note to the project review. She schedules the presentation feature for the next iteration and explains the trade-off to her manager.

The outcome may still be disappointing. The manager may reward the launch more. The repair may take longer than expected. Value creation does not control those results. It changes the standard Mina is willing to enact and defend.

So far, we have seen that self-authorship is not a private declaration of uniqueness. It is the work of making a value visible in repeated choices while accepting that other values, other people, and the world will push back.

Self-Authorship Needs Friction

Nietzsche's language of individuality can sound like an invitation to make oneself exceptional. Used carelessly, it becomes vanity: “My intensity proves that I am beyond ordinary obligations.”

Friction is the corrective. A value is tested when it costs time, conflicts with another value, affects someone else, or fails to win approval. Mina's commitment has friction because it may reduce short-term recognition, requires a difficult conversation, and makes her answerable to the users affected by the product.

This does not mean that every painful choice is noble. Suffering, risk, and intensity are not automatic evidence of depth. The relevant question is whether the value produces a life and practice Mina can examine honestly, revise when needed, and defend without reducing other people to props in her self-image.

Answer: The source gives Mina a reason to inspect the value; it does not by itself prove that visibility has no value. She still needs to ask what the rule rewards, what it hides, and whether a revised commitment fits the work and people involved.

Check: Mina says, “I create my own values, so I do not need to explain why an accessibility repair can wait forever.” What has she confused?

Think first, then reveal.

Answer: She has confused value creation with arbitrary preference. A created value must take form in practice, face consequences, and answer to the commitments and people it affects. Self-authorship is not exemption from accountability.

Trade-offs and Limits

The central trade-off is that examining inherited values can free a person from an automatic script, but it removes the comfort of treating familiar praise, guilt, or status as a complete guide.

This helps when a standard feels compulsory even though it repeatedly narrows a person's actions or harms something they also value. It costs attention, uncertainty, and the risk that a person will discover a conflict they can no longer ignore.

Genealogy does not prove that a value is false, supply a complete moral theory, or reveal every motive with psychological certainty. It can also become a cynical weapon: a person may explain every opponent's value as hidden resentment while refusing to examine their own incentives.

You can see the boundary when suspicion becomes the whole practice. If no value can survive examination, the learner has stopped auditing values and started treating detachment as a value above all others.

Practice: Audit a Borrowed Standard

Jonas is offered a prestigious leadership role. It requires leaving a community music group he promised to support for the next six months. He says:

Important people always take the bigger role. The music group will understand.

Make a five-part value audit:

  1. state the inherited standard;
  2. name one source or incentive that may make it attractive;
  3. identify one real good the standard protects;
  4. identify one cost or value it may hide;
  5. draft one revised commitment and one action that would test it.

A good answer should mention:

  • Inherited standard: status and larger titles prove importance.
  • Source or incentive: career culture, fear of being overlooked, money, praise, or a genuine desire for wider influence.
  • Real good: the role may provide resources, learning, or a chance to help more people.
  • Hidden cost: Jonas may treat a promise and collaborators as disposable or make status the only measure of a valuable life.
  • Revised commitment: for example, pursue larger responsibility without silently abandoning existing commitments.
  • Test action: speak to the music group, negotiate a transition, find a replacement, or decline openly after comparing the real costs.

The practice does not declare that Jonas must refuse the role. It asks him to replace an inherited slogan with a commitment he can enact and answer for.

Connection to the Next Lesson

Stoicism examined the judgment that follows an event. Nietzsche adds a longer question: which values trained that judgment to feel obvious in the first place?

The next lesson moves from personal self-authorship to responsibility under leverage. When a person's values shape a tool that affects other people, private sincerity is not enough; consequences, consent, accountability, and repair enter the design.

Key Takeaways

  • A genealogy asks where a value gained authority, what it rewards, what it hides, and what it does in a life.
  • Finding a value's origin is not enough to refute it; the point is to make its incentives and effects available for judgment.
  • Nietzschean value creation is not arbitrary preference. It is a difficult, constrained practice of giving commitments form over time.
  • Self-authorship needs friction: real costs, conflict between values, other people's claims, and a willingness to revise.
  • Suspicion is useful only when it leads to a better, more accountable practice rather than permanent cynicism.
