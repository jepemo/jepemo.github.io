The Existential Problem: Freedom Without a Script

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to...

  • Separate given conditions, inherited scripts, uncertainties, possibilities, and chosen commitments in a difficult decision.

  • Explain why existential freedom is situated freedom, not unlimited control.

  • Recognize two forms of evasion: pretending a role determines the choice and pretending constraints do not matter.

Idea in one sentence: You do not choose the whole situation, but your actions still help decide what the situation will mean and who you are becoming within it.

Core Insight

Nora receives an offer for a job in another city. The work matters to her, and the salary would give her more security. But her father needs regular help after an illness. Nora has some savings, but not enough to move twice if the new job fails.

She wants someone to tell her the correct choice.

One answer is ready immediately:

A good daughter stays.

Another answer arrives just as quickly:

A serious person always takes the bigger opportunity.

Both answers feel like instructions. Neither one has yet examined Nora's actual situation. Each turns a social role into a script that appears to decide for her.

Existentialist thought begins at this uncomfortable point. Nora did not choose her father's illness, the offer's deadline, her financial limits, or the expectations she learned. Those conditions are real. Yet no list of conditions can finish the decision. Nora must still interpret what matters, consider possibilities, act, and accept that the action expresses a commitment.

Freedom here is not magic power. It is the lack of a complete script.

The Temptation to Discover a Fixed Answer

When a choice is painful, we often search for a fact that will remove the need to choose.

Nora might say:

  • “I am a daughter, so staying is not a choice.”
  • “This is a rare offer, so accepting it is not a choice.”
  • “I feel anxious, so leaving must be wrong.”
  • “My friends agree that I should go, so the responsibility is theirs.”

Each sentence mixes a real feature of the situation with an interpretation.

Her relationship to her father is real. The job offer is real. Her anxiety is real. Her friends' advice is real. But none of these facts contains a complete command.

The initial model is:

facts about me + facts about the world -> one correct role -> required action

This model is attractive because it promises innocence. If the role chose the action, Nora does not have to own what the action sacrifices.

The model breaks because the same facts can support more than one serious response. Nora could move, decline, negotiate a later start, ask relatives to share care, arrange paid support, or propose remote work. Some options may fail. Some may be unaffordable. But discovering that requires inquiry and action, not merely naming her role.

Facticity and Possibility

Existentialist thinkers do not form one unified school. This lesson uses a practical distinction associated especially with Sartre and Beauvoir: we are always both situated and able to take a position toward our situation.

Plain meaning:

You begin inside conditions you did not fully choose.

In Nora's situation:

Her history, body, money, relationships, and the offer's deadline shape what she can realistically do.

Technical name:

These given conditions are often called facticity. Facticity includes facts about the past and present that cannot be erased by wishing.

But Nora is not only a list of facts. She can imagine alternatives, question inherited meanings, make plans, and act toward a future that does not exist yet. Sartrean vocabulary calls this movement beyond what is already given transcendence. In this lesson, the simpler word will be possibility.

The stronger model is therefore:

given conditions
      +
interpreted possibilities
      +
chosen commitment
      ->
action with consequences

Neither side can be deleted. Facticity without possibility turns a person into a fixed object. Possibility without facticity turns freedom into fantasy.

Build a Constraint-and-Commitment Map

Before deciding, Nora separates five different kinds of statement.

Statement Kind Why the distinction matters
The offer expires on Friday. Given condition The deadline is part of the present situation. Denying it removes a real constraint.
My father currently needs help three evenings each week. Given condition The need affects other people and cannot be replaced by a slogan about independence.
A good daughter never moves away. Inherited script This is an interpretation of care, not a physical law. It can be examined.
If I decline, I will never get meaningful work again. Uncertainty presented as fact Nora does not know this. Treating a prediction as certain closes possibilities too early.
I could ask for a delayed start or two remote days. Possibility The option may be refused, but it becomes visible only when Nora tests the situation.
I want to care for my father without making care the whole definition of either life. Possible commitment This states something Nora may choose to stand for. It still needs a concrete action.

This map does not calculate the correct answer. Its job is smaller and more useful: it stops facts, predictions, social expectations, options, and commitments from pretending to be the same thing.

A Worked Decision Path

Now follow Nora's reasoning from pressure to action.

Starting point: “I have no choice”

Nora first says she must decline. The sentence hides both the care constraint and her fear of being judged.

Step 1: Record facticity without argument

She writes only what is presently given:

  • the offer expires Friday;
  • the new city is three hours away;
  • her father needs help on Monday, Wednesday, and Friday;
  • one sibling lives nearby but has not been asked to help;
  • Nora can afford one move, not a long period without income.

The list does not include “good daughter” or “last chance.” Those are interpretations.

Step 2: Expose the scripts

Nora writes two competing scripts:

Care script: If I leave, I am abandoning my father.
Success script: If I stay, I am wasting my life.

The scripts disagree, but they share a structure. Both reduce Nora to a role and hide the need to decide what care and meaningful work will require in this situation.

Step 3: Generate possibilities that respect the facts

Nora does not ask, “What could I do in a world without limits?” She asks, “What can I test from here?”

She identifies four actions:

  1. Decline immediately and keep the current care arrangement.
  2. Accept immediately and assume the family will adapt.
  3. Ask the employer for a delayed start and two remote days.
  4. Ask her sibling and father to design a shared care plan before Friday.

Options 3 and 4 require conversations. They may produce new information. Freedom includes this work of opening or closing real possibilities.

Step 4: State commitments before choosing

Nora names three commitments:

  • treat her father as a participant, not as a burden to manage;
  • avoid silently assigning all care to herself or her sibling;
  • pursue meaningful work without pretending that ambition cancels relationships.

These commitments do not remove conflict. They make the conflict more honest.

Step 5: Act and own the remainder

Nora asks for the delayed start and arranges a family conversation. Suppose the employer accepts the delay but refuses remote work. The family can cover two evenings, but Friday remains unresolved.

Nora now chooses with better information. If she accepts, she owns the remaining care problem and must keep working on it. If she declines, she owns the cost to her project and cannot describe the result as something her role mechanically forced.

The output is not certainty. It is an owned commitment under constraints.

The naive path would have converted one script directly into action. The stronger path made intermediate states visible: facts, scripts, uncertainties, tested possibilities, commitments, and consequences.

So far, we have seen that existential freedom does not erase Nora's situation. It changes how she relates to it. She cannot choose every available outcome, but she can refuse to let a role, prediction, or feeling impersonate a complete command.

Anxiety Is Not a Decision Procedure

A decision without a guaranteed script can produce anxiety. This makes sense. Nora sees that each serious option closes another possibility and affects people she cares about.

Anxiety can reveal that the choice matters and that no external rule will remove all responsibility. It does not prove that a choice is wrong. It also does not prove that a choice is profound or authentic.

Treat anxiety as information about stakes, not as an oracle.

Check: Nora feels calm after deciding to decline because her family approves. Does the calm prove the decision is authentic?

Think first, then reveal.

Answer: No. Approval may reduce anxiety without showing whether Nora examined the facts, scripts, possibilities, and costs. Authenticity is not a feeling score. The relevant question is whether she owns the choice lucidly rather than hiding it behind a role.

Two Ways to Evade Freedom

Sartre uses bad faith for forms of self-deception in which a person tries to escape the tension between facticity and possibility. Two patterns are especially useful here.

Evasion 1: “I am only this role”

Nora treats “daughter” as a fixed essence that determines every action.

Why it is tempting:

The role supplies a script and moves responsibility elsewhere.

Better model:

The relationship is real, and it creates serious claims on Nora. But what good care requires still has to be interpreted and enacted with her father and others.

Evasion 2: “Facts cannot limit the real me”

Nora treats freedom as permission to leave without planning because any constraint would be inauthentic.

Why it is tempting:

Pure possibility feels bold. It avoids the slow work of money, bodies, promises, and other people's agency.

Better model:

A choice becomes real through a situation. Denying consequences is not freedom; it is a refusal to see what the action does.

Check: Which statement better expresses situated freedom?

  1. “I can choose any outcome if I commit strongly enough.”
  2. “I cannot guarantee the outcome, but I can examine the situation, test possibilities, and own the action I take.”

Think first, then reveal.

Answer: Statement 2. Freedom concerns interpretation, commitment, and action within conditions. It does not grant control over other people, institutions, chance, or results.

Freedom Is Social and Unequal

The language of freedom can become cruel when it ignores power. People do not begin with equal money, safety, legal status, health, time, or social permission. Oppression can shrink which possibilities are visible, safe, or materially available.

Saying that a person still takes a position toward a situation must not become a way to blame them for the situation. Nora's agency does not cause her father's illness, create affordable care, or make the employer flexible. Her choice also involves other freedoms: her father and sibling are subjects with projects of their own, not pieces in Nora's private story.

This boundary matters. A useful existential analysis asks both questions:

Which conditions restrict action here?
What response is still being chosen, delegated, postponed, or refused?

Leaving out the first question produces fantasy or victim-blaming. Leaving out the second can hide the agency that remains.

Trade-offs and Limits

The facticity–possibility distinction improves clarity. It reveals where a fact ends and an interpretation begins. It also makes hidden commitments visible.

The central trade-off is that greater ownership of a choice brings greater clarity, but removes the comfort of pretending that a role, prediction, or adviser made the decision.

It costs comfort. A person may have to admit that no option preserves every value and that refusing to decide is itself part of the unfolding situation.

The distinction does not settle every moral conflict. It cannot calculate what Nora owes her father, guarantee a fair care arrangement, or prove that the job deserves priority. Ethical reasoning, dialogue, material support, and institutional change still matter.

You can see the boundary when “own your choice” starts replacing attention to coercion, dependency, poverty, disability, or other people's freedom. At that point, the vocabulary of agency is hiding the situation instead of clarifying it.

Practice: Separate the Layers

Leo promised to help a neighborhood food cooperative every Thursday for three months. A prestigious course is then scheduled for the same evening. Leo says:

Ambitious people take rare opportunities. I have no choice but to leave the cooperative.

Build a five-row map for Leo:

  1. one given condition;
  2. one inherited script;
  3. one uncertainty;
  4. two possibilities;
  5. one commitment that could constrain the final action.

Then write one action Leo could take before deciding.

A good answer should mention:

  • Given condition: the schedules conflict and Leo made a three-month promise.
  • Inherited script: “Ambitious people always take prestigious opportunities.”
  • Uncertainty: the course may or may not be unique, useful, or impossible to take later.
  • Possibilities: ask about recordings or another cohort; discuss a replacement or transition with the cooperative; decline one commitment openly.
  • Commitment: for example, pursue growth without treating other people as disposable.
  • Action before decision: gather information and speak to the people affected rather than letting the status of the course decide silently.

The rubric does not require Leo to stay or leave. It requires him to distinguish the layers and own what his action expresses.

Key Takeaways

  • Facticity names given conditions; possibility names the capacity to interpret, project, test, and act beyond what is already settled.
  • Existential freedom is situated. It does not mean unlimited options or control over outcomes.
  • An inherited role, a prediction, or a feeling can shape a choice without mechanically deciding it.
  • Bad faith can deny possibility by hiding behind a fixed role, or deny facticity by pretending constraints and consequences do not matter.
  • A responsible choice makes facts, commitments, effects on others, and unresolved costs visible even when certainty is impossible.
