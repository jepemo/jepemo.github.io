CAP, PACELC, and Partition-Time Behavior

Core Insight

Imagine a ticketing system with one seat left for a concert. The system runs in two regions so users near each region get fast responses. Then the network link between the regions breaks. Region A can no longer talk to region B, but both regions are still receiving purchase attempts.

At that moment, the system cannot keep every nice promise at once. If both regions keep selling the last seat, the product may promise the same seat to two people. If one region refuses to sell until communication returns, some users see errors or delay even though their local region is alive.

That is the practical heart of CAP. During a partition, when groups of nodes cannot communicate reliably, a distributed system must choose which promise to weaken for a specific operation. It can preserve a single consistent answer by rejecting or delaying uncertain work, or it can keep more requests available by accepting local work that may need repair later.

PACELC adds the normal-day version of the same pressure. If there is a partition, choose between availability and consistency. Else, even without a partition, choose between lower latency and stronger consistency. The letters matter less than the review habit: name what the system does during failure and what it pays during health.

The Partition Moment

Use the last-seat purchase as the worked example. Before the partition, both regions can coordinate:

region A <----replication----> region B

concert-9:
  remaining_seats = 1

Now the link breaks:

region A     X     region B

request in A: buy the last seat
request in B: buy the last seat

Both regions are alive. Both have users waiting. Neither region can ask the other, "Did you already sell this seat?" The product promise decides what should happen next.

If the promise is "never sell one seat twice," the system should not let both sides accept the purchase independently. It may route all writes through one side, require a quorum, reject uncertain purchases, or place the seat in a degraded mode until coordination is possible again.

If the state is a shopping cart item instead, the system might choose differently. Adding a book to a cart in two regions can often be merged later. The user-visible damage of temporary divergence is much lower than promising the same scarce seat to two people.

CAP is therefore not a slogan about a whole company or database. It is a question about one data path under partition: what behavior protects the promise attached to this state?

CAP Vocabulary

CAP uses three words that are easy to overgeneralize.

Consistency, in this CAP discussion, means the protected operation behaves as if there is one up-to-date answer. For the last seat, readers and writers should not observe two incompatible official versions of remaining_seats.

Availability means a request to a non-failing node receives a non-error response. A system that says "try again later" may be doing the right product thing, but under the technical CAP definition it has given up availability for that request.

Partition tolerance means the system has defined behavior when messages between groups are lost or delayed. In real distributed systems, partitions are not optional. The network can fail, links can become one-way, queues can stall, and regions can become isolated. Once that happens, the choice is not "partition or no partition." The choice is what the operation does while the partition exists.

The useful version is:

if replicas cannot communicate:
  preserve one official answer and reject or delay some work
  or accept local work and allow temporary divergence

Neither choice is always correct. The correct choice depends on whether divergence can be merged without violating the promise.

Different State, Different Choice

The same system can make different partition-time choices for different state.

A bank withdrawal, scarce seat, lock, or configuration publish may need a strong rule. If two partitions make independent official decisions, the system may create obligations it cannot honor. These paths often prefer consistency over availability during partition.

A shopping cart, draft document, notification count, or analytics counter may tolerate local progress. The system can accept writes in both partitions and merge, deduplicate, or reconcile later. These paths may prefer availability during partition because the domain has a safe repair story.

The practical review question is:

If both sides accept work during a partition,
can we merge the results without breaking the promise?

If the answer is yes, local availability may be reasonable. If the answer is no, the system should probably wait, reject, route through an owner, or require coordination.

This is also where degraded modes become useful. A ticketing system might continue showing event information while disabling final purchase. A banking app might show the last known balance while refusing withdrawals. A cart service might accept additions but delay checkout. Those behaviors are not generic "down" states. They are product-specific choices about which promises remain safe.

PACELC On A Normal Day

CAP focuses attention on partitions. PACELC reminds us that coordination has a cost even when the network is healthy.

The shorthand is:

P: if there is a partition,
   choose A or C

ELC: else,
     choose between latency and consistency

For the ticketing system, a stronger normal-day design might wait for a remote quorum before confirming a purchase. That improves confidence that only one region sold the final seat, but it adds latency to every purchase. A lower-latency design might confirm locally and replicate afterward, but then it needs a very clear answer for what happens if communication fails at the wrong moment.

For a product catalog, the choice may be different. Serving a slightly stale product description from a nearby replica may be acceptable because the user experience benefits from speed and the business cost of short-lived staleness is low.

PACELC keeps the trade-off visible outside outages. Stronger freshness usually spends coordination, distance, and waiting. Lower latency often spends freshness, recency, or a narrower promise about what the response means.

Failure Modes And Trade-offs

The obvious failure is overselling: two partitions both accept the last-seat purchase, and repair later cannot make both promises true. A refund may compensate financially, but the system still broke the product promise.

A quieter failure is pretending that all state has the same CAP choice. A company may call a database "available" or "consistent," but a real product usually has many data paths. Password changes, search indexes, carts, counters, ledgers, and feeds do not need identical behavior.

Another failure is returning a response without naming its strength. "Saved" after a local-only write means something different from "saved after quorum." If the product treats both messages the same, users and operators will misunderstand what the system has promised.

The main trade-off is honest but uncomfortable: during partition, preserving a single official answer may make some users wait; accepting local work may keep the interface moving but create divergence that must be safe to repair.

Key Takeaways

  • CAP is most useful as a question about one operation's behavior during a partition.
  • During partition, unsafe divergence must be prevented by waiting, rejecting, ownership, or coordination.
  • Different state can make different availability and consistency choices.
  • PACELC adds the normal-day trade-off between lower latency and stronger freshness.
