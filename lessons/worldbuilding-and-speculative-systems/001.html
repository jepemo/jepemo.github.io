Worlds Start With Constraints

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to...

  • Distinguish a decorative world fact from a constraint that changes what people can do.

  • Trace a constraint through choices, adaptations, dependencies, and conflict.

  • Design one small constraint that creates several coherent consequences without explaining the whole world.

Idea in one sentence: A world starts to feel real when a limit blocks the easy choice and forces people to adapt.

Core Insight

Imagine the city of Nima. It travels on the back of a vast stone animal.

Every thirty days, the animal walks through a freshwater valley. For six hours, the people of Nima can lower pipes and fill their cisterns. Then the city moves on.

That six-hour window is not just a curious fact. It changes daily life.

The city needs storage. Someone must maintain the pipes. Work stops during collection. Families argue about priority. A damaged cistern is not a small repair problem; it threatens the next twenty-four days.

One limit has produced technology, work, authority, inequality, risk, and possible stories.

This is the foundation of constraint-driven worldbuilding:

Do not begin by asking how many facts the world contains. Begin by asking what pushes back when someone wants something.

A Fact Is Not Yet a Constraint

A world fact describes something.

Nima has three moons.

That may become important, but it is not automatically a constraint. If the moons do not change light, tides, calendars, travel, belief, or any choice, they are decoration.

A constraint describes resistance.

Nima can collect fresh water for only six hours every thirty days.

Now a person wants something—water at any time—and the world refuses the easy path. The person must choose, wait, pay, cooperate, steal, invent, or go without.

Plain meaning:

A constraint is a limit that makes at least one desired action impossible, costly, risky, delayed, or dependent on someone else.

In Nima:

People cannot collect water whenever they wish. They depend on storage, timing, labor, and maintenance.

Technical name:

This is a world constraint. It creates a stable pressure from which other parts of the world can grow.

The key word is not limit. The key word is choice. A mountain on a map is only a fact until it changes travel, defense, trade, weather, communication, or belonging.

The Naive Approach: Add More Lore

When a world feels thin, the tempting response is to add more things:

  • twelve noble houses
  • seven holidays
  • a lost empire
  • glowing animals
  • a secret alphabet
  • names for every district

None of these is bad. The problem is that a list does not explain why anyone behaves as they do.

Suppose Nima also has blue towers, silver masks, and a thousand-year water festival. If removing those details changes no decision, they are still loose decoration.

Now connect the festival to the water window. It begins when the pipes descend. Masks mark which maintenance crew may enter each cistern. The oldest tower holds the city clock, and a wrong signal can waste the collection window.

The details have not become better because they are more elaborate. They have become connected to pressure.

Check: Which statement is already a constraint?

A. The capital has gates made of green glass. B. The gates magnify midday heat, so they cannot be opened safely for two hours. C. The capital was founded 900 years ago.

Think first, then reveal.

Answer: B. It blocks an action at a specific time and forces travelers, guards, and merchants to adapt. A and C may later matter, but they do not yet change a choice.

The Constraint Chain

A useful constraint does not need to explain everything. It needs to create a traceable chain.

Use this sequence:

condition
  -> blocked easy choice
  -> adaptation
  -> new dependency
  -> visible consequence

Let us trace Nima step by step.

Stage Question Nima's answer
Condition What does the world permit or deny? Fresh water is reachable for six hours every thirty days.
Desire What does someone need or want? Households need reliable water between valley crossings.
Blocked easy choice Why can they not get it directly? The moving city leaves the valley, and no other source is known.
Adaptation What do people build or practice? They build cisterns, schedule collection shifts, reuse water, and inspect pipes.
Dependency What must now keep working? The city depends on clocks, storage, repair crews, records, and allocation rules.
Uneven effect Who gains or loses? Upper districts receive pressure first; damaged lower pipes lose water sooner.
Story pressure What decision becomes difficult? After a cistern cracks, leaders must choose which district receives the remaining reserve.

The intermediate steps matter. If we jump from “water is scarce” directly to “there is a rebellion,” the world may still feel arbitrary. The table shows the machinery between the limit and the conflict.

So far, we have seen that one physical condition can create social consequences. The condition does not mechanically determine one culture or one plot. It creates a field of costly choices.

Four Lenses for Finding Constraints

You can inspect a world through four lenses. They are prompts, not boxes that every world must fill.

Physical constraints

What do bodies, terrain, climate, distance, time, or physics make difficult?

In Nima, the city moves and the water window is short.

Resource and access constraints

What is scarce, expensive, controlled, perishable, or available only at certain times?

In Nima, stored water and pipe access are limited. The next lesson will examine this material layer in more depth.

Social constraints

What rules, customs, institutions, promises, or power relations restrict action?

In Nima, a household may be physically able to open a cistern but legally forbidden to do so. A rule can be broken, but breaking it has a cost.

Narrative and knowledge constraints

What is unknown, hidden, disputed, irreversible, or urgent for the people making decisions?

Perhaps nobody knows whether the stone animal will return to the valley after an injury. That uncertainty changes planning even before the route changes.

These lenses interact. A strong world often begins when a physical limit becomes a resource problem, a resource problem creates an institution, and the institution controls information.

Trade-offs: Hard Limits and Soft Pressure

Not every constraint says “impossible.”

A hard limit closes a path:

Once Nima leaves the valley, its pipes cannot reach the water.

A soft constraint keeps the path open but gives it a cost:

A family may buy emergency water, but the price is one month of wages.

Hard limits create clear boundaries. Soft constraints create choices between bad options. Most believable worlds use both.

If every limit is hard, characters may have no meaningful choice. If every limit disappears when inconvenient, choices lose weight.

The design trade-off is focus versus freedom. A strong constraint makes consequences easier to trace, but each added constraint removes possible actions and adds facts the creator must remember. Use a few connected limits. Add another only when it creates a new kind of choice rather than repeating the same pressure.

Constraints Create Possibility Too

Constraint sounds negative, but a useful limit is also a creative engine.

Because Nima cannot collect water every day, its people may invent:

  • folding condensation cloths
  • public wash schedules
  • songs that measure pipe-lowering time
  • sealed cups used as contracts
  • laws about hidden household tanks
  • rituals for the first drink after collection

The limit narrows one path and opens many others.

This is why “anything can happen” often produces less wonder than a bounded world. When anything can happen, an event explains little. When the learner knows the pressure, a small exception, invention, or betrayal carries meaning.

Common Confusions

Confusion: More rules make a world more believable

Why it is tempting:

Rules look like evidence of depth.

Better model:

A rule earns its place when it changes choices or consequences. Ten disconnected rules create homework. One connected constraint creates a system.

Confusion: A constraint determines one inevitable culture

Why it is tempting:

The causal chain can look like a straight line.

Better model:

The same pressure can produce cooperation, markets, religious duty, authoritarian control, local independence, or several responses at once. Constraints bound possibilities; they do not remove human variation.

Confusion: Consistency requires explaining everything

Why it is tempting:

Unanswered questions can feel like gaps.

Better model:

The creator needs to know enough to preserve consequences. The audience does not need a manual for every pipe. Mystery is compatible with constraints as long as mystery does not become a convenient escape from them.

Confusion: A late rescue is surprising, so it is good worldbuilding

Why it is tempting:

Unexpected solutions create excitement.

Better model:

A surprise should reveal a hidden cost, capability, or assumption. If rain appears only because the cistern cracked, the limit was not real. If rain can be summoned by sacrificing the city's only navigation stone, the rescue creates a new choice and a new consequence.

Check the Chain

Check: Nima's leaders solve every shortage by creating water with a spell that has no cost, limit, owner, or side effect. What happens to the original constraint?

Think first, then reveal.

Answer: It disappears. The spell may be visually impressive, but it restores the easy choice. To preserve pressure, the spell needs a boundary that matters: limited users, uncertain results, a scarce input, political control, or a damaging consequence.

Practice: Build One Pressure Chain

Consider this seed:

In the Glass Orchard, fruit becomes poisonous one hour after it is picked.

Write a five-step chain:

  1. Name one person or group that wants something.
  2. Show which easy choice the limit blocks.
  3. Invent one adaptation.
  4. Name one new dependency created by that adaptation.
  5. End with one difficult decision or visible consequence.

A good answer should keep the original limit active. It should not solve the poison with a free cure.

One model answer:

Traveling merchants want to sell the fruit in distant towns. Ordinary transport is too slow. Orchard workers build mobile kitchens and preserve the fruit before the hour ends. The trade now depends on trained preservers and accurate harvest bells. When the bell tower fails, the guild must choose whether to destroy a valuable harvest or risk selling poisoned jars.

Notice the chain: limit, blocked transport, adaptation, dependency, decision. We still know almost nothing about the orchard's wider history. We know enough to begin asking useful questions.

Key Takeaways

  • A fact becomes a constraint when it blocks an easy choice and forces adaptation.
  • Trace constraints through intermediate steps: condition, blocked choice, adaptation, dependency, and consequence.
  • Physical, resource, social, and knowledge constraints often combine, but they do not determine one inevitable culture.
  • A useful limit creates possibilities as well as restrictions.
  • Add fewer constraints and make their consequences visible; unexplained abundance is not the same as depth.
