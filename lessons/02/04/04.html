Day 039: Writing and Reviewing Architecture Proposals

Architecture work becomes useful when the reasoning is written down clearly enough that other engineers can challenge it, extend it, and operate it.

Today's "Aha!" Moment

The insight: A useful architecture proposal is not a polished slide deck. It is a structured explanation of the problem, the options, the chosen design, and the trade-offs the team is accepting consciously.

Why this matters: Many design efforts fail socially, not technically. The system could work, but the proposal does not make assumptions, constraints, or failure behavior visible enough for the team to evaluate it.

Concrete anchor: The learning platform team wants to redesign progress tracking and notifications. Several options could work, but the real need is a document that lets reviewers understand the problem, challenge the assumptions, and see how the design behaves under failure.

The universal pattern: Problem framing + options + chosen trade-offs + reviewable reasoning -> architecture proposal that improves team judgment.

How to recognize when this applies:

  • A migration or redesign is being proposed.
  • Multiple teams will operate or depend on the new system.
  • Reviewers are debating tools because the problem statement is fuzzy.
  • Diagrams exist, but the document does not say what was rejected or why.
  • Operational consequences are not visible in the proposal.

Common misconceptions:

  • [INCORRECT] "A proposal is successful if the diagram looks complete."
  • [INCORRECT] "Reviewers only need the chosen solution, not the alternatives."
  • [CORRECT] The truth: Strong architecture writing makes the decision path inspectable, including assumptions, rejected options, and expected failure modes.

Real-world examples:

  1. Service extraction proposals: The team needs to understand why a boundary helps more than it hurts.
  2. Database migrations: The operational risk matters as much as the target technology.
  3. Event-driven redesigns: Reviewers need clarity on consistency, retries, and observability implications.
  4. Global rollout plans: A proposal must explain failure containment and rollback, not just the happy path.

Why This Matters

The problem: Architecture decisions often live in fragmented conversations, partial diagrams, and unwritten assumptions.

Before:

  • Reviews revolve around opinions instead of explicit trade-offs.
  • Important assumptions remain implicit.
  • The implementation team inherits ambiguity and hidden risk.

After:

  • The design problem is framed clearly.
  • Alternatives and rejected paths are visible.
  • Reviewers can evaluate failure paths, rollout strategy, and operating costs early.

Real-world impact: Better proposals reduce rework, make reviews faster, improve onboarding, and create more durable engineering memory.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this session, you will be able to:

  1. Structure an architecture proposal - Organize problem framing, constraints, options, and chosen design coherently.
  2. Make trade-offs reviewable - Expose why a design was chosen and what it costs.
  3. Improve design reviews - Evaluate proposals through failure paths, alternatives, and operational consequences.

Core Concepts Explained

Concept 1: Start with the Problem and the Constraints

Concrete example / mini-scenario: The progress-tracking redesign is failing because updates arrive late across devices and downstream notifications are unreliable during bursts. That is a much better opening than "we propose using a queue and a new database."

What it is: The first section of a proposal should define the problem, the user or business impact, the constraints, and the non-goals.

Why it matters: Reviewers can only evaluate a solution if they understand what the solution is trying to protect and what it is intentionally not solving.

How it works: Write the current pain, the desired outcome, the invariants, the expected scale, and the explicit boundaries of the proposal.

Mental model: If the reader cannot explain the problem after the first page, the rest of the proposal will be misread.

When to use it:

  • [Ideal situation] Any RFC, migration plan, or major service redesign.
  • [Anti-pattern] Opening with tool choices before the problem is explained.

Concept 2: Show the Happy Path and the Failure Path

Concrete example / mini-scenario: For progress tracking, the proposal should show how an update flows normally and what happens if the event bus is slow, the notification service is down, or the client retries.

What it is: Architecture documentation should include both the intended request or event flow and the main degraded states.

Why it matters: Many designs look reasonable until you ask what happens when a dependency is slow, duplicated, or unavailable.

How it works: Include one main flow, one failure flow, and one rollout or recovery note. This is usually enough to reveal the most important trade-offs.

Mental model: A building plan is incomplete if it shows the front door but not the fire exits.

Code Example (If applicable):

proposal = {
    "problem": "Progress updates are inconsistent across devices",
    "constraints": ["sub-second UX target", "retries are common"],
    "chosen_design": "append events, update read model asynchronously",
    "failure_path": "if notifier is down, persist event and retry later",
}

Note: The value is not in the syntax. The value is in making the decision structure explicit and reviewable.

When to use it:

  • [Ideal situation] Proposals that affect reliability, consistency, or user-facing latency.
  • [Anti-pattern] Documenting only the happy path and leaving degradation behavior to implementation guesswork.

Concept 3: Good Reviews Evaluate Alternatives, Not Just the Chosen Diagram

Concrete example / mini-scenario: Reviewers should ask why the team chose asynchronous notifications over tightly coupled synchronous calls, and what cost is being accepted in consistency and complexity.

What it is: A design review is strongest when the rejected options are briefly described and the trade-off boundary is made explicit.

Why it matters: This prevents hindsight arguments later and shows that the chosen design was selected intentionally rather than by default.

How it works: List the main alternative, say why it was not chosen, and describe the operational or product reason behind that choice.

Mental model: A useful review is a comparison exercise, not a ceremony for approving the first visible diagram.

When to use it:

  • [Ideal situation] Cross-team reviews, architecture boards, and major roadmap decisions.
  • [Anti-pattern] Asking only whether the chosen design can work, instead of whether it is the best fit among reasonable options.

Troubleshooting

Issue: Writing proposals that are mostly diagrams and jargon.

Why it happens / is confusing: Visuals feel efficient, but without problem framing and trade-off notes, reviewers fill the gaps with their own assumptions.

Clarification / Fix: Add plain-language sections for the problem, constraints, non-goals, chosen trade-offs, and primary failure path.

Issue: Treating review comments as noise.

Why it happens / is confusing: Design feedback can feel subjective when the proposal does not state assumptions explicitly.

Clarification / Fix: Use review comments to expose hidden assumptions and improve the clarity of the decision record.

Advanced Connections

Connection 1: Architecture Proposals ↔ ADRs

The parallel: Architecture proposals are broader than single ADRs, but both aim to preserve why a decision was made, not just what was built.

Real-world case: Teams often summarize large proposals into smaller ADRs as implementation decisions become more focused.

Connection 2: Design Reviews ↔ Incident Learning

The parallel: Good incident reviews and good architecture reviews both ask which assumptions were wrong and which failure paths were insufficiently modeled.

Real-world case: A proposal that already documented degradation behavior usually makes later incident analysis much clearer.

Key Insights

  1. A proposal is a reasoning artifact - Its job is to make the decision path visible, not just to display a chosen architecture.
  2. Failure paths deserve equal space with happy paths - Review quality improves when degraded behavior is documented early.
  3. Alternatives make decisions credible - A design is easier to trust when the rejected options and their trade-offs are explicit.

Knowledge Check (Test Questions)

  1. What should come first in a strong architecture proposal?

    • A) The full technology stack.
    • B) The problem, constraints, and non-goals.
    • C) A detailed cost spreadsheet.

  2. Why should proposals include a failure path?

    • A) Because systems are only evaluated on the happy path.
    • B) Because important trade-offs often become visible only when a dependency is slow or unavailable.
    • C) Because reviewers prefer longer documents.

  3. What makes a design review more meaningful?

    • A) Showing why the chosen option beat at least one reasonable alternative.
    • B) Avoiding mention of rejected options so the review stays focused.
    • C) Limiting feedback to diagram aesthetics.

Answers

1. B: Reviewers need the problem and constraints first. Without that context, the rest of the proposal is easy to misinterpret.

2. B: Many real trade-offs emerge under degraded conditions, so failure paths are central to honest architectural reasoning.

3. A: Alternatives prove the team evaluated real trade-offs rather than merely documenting the first workable idea.
