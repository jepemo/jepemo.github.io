Capstone: Build a Comparative Reading Dossier

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to...

  • Compare two passages through formal choices rather than theme matching alone.

  • Build an evidence ledger that separates observation, inference, open questions, and unsupported speculation.

  • Present a defensible comparative claim with counterreading, limits, and a revision path.

Idea in one sentence: A comparative dossier makes two texts answer the same question while showing exactly how their different narrative choices produce different meanings.

Core Insight

The track has supplied the tools; the capstone turns them into an artifact. You will not write two plot summaries or rank which text is “deeper.” You will ask one shared question, collect comparable evidence, explain each passage's mechanism, and show where the comparison stops.

For a concrete task, compare two invented passages about how a community decides what counts as truth when someone is absent. One uses a realistic archive and a contested record. The other uses a village rule in which a bell marks an absent person's door. Their subject overlaps, but their forms do different work.

The dossier should let another reader audit your reasoning:

shared question -> evidence ledger -> passage-by-passage claims -> comparison -> counterreading -> bounded conclusion

The Two Passages

Passage A: The Orchard Register

At 07:10, Mara's brother Ivo found her green scarf on the orchard gate. The gate had been locked since the frost, according to the notice nailed to the post. The registrar said Mara had taken the south road at dawn and wrote voluntary departure in the book.

Ivo remembered Mara saying the orchard was “closed only to people who believed the sign.” At noon, he found a receipt for a bus ticket dated 06:40. The registrar refused to show the departure log. By evening, the scarf had been removed from the gate, but a wet line of soil led from the gate to the archive door.

Passage B: The Bell House

In Bell House village, a doorbell rings once when a resident leaves and twice when the village has agreed not to speak the resident's name. When Sela's father disappears, the bell rings twice at 05:00. The village head says the second ring is only an old mechanism.

Sela records the sound in her notebook. Three days later, the same bell rings once while her father's coat is still hanging inside. Her neighbor tells her that a name can be absent from speech without being absent from a house. At the end of the week, Sela hears a third ring from her own door and tears the page from the notebook.

Do not assume the passages share a world rule. The comparison asks what each text makes visible about absence, evidence, and agency.

Step 1: Define the Shared Question and Scope

A useful question is narrow enough to answer with formal evidence:

How do the two passages turn a missing person into a problem of public knowledge, and how do their different rules distribute agency to the searching character?

Avoid questions that can be answered with a theme word:

  • Weak: “How do both passages show loss?”
  • Stronger: “How do records, repeated sounds, and withheld testimony change what the searcher can know and do?”

Write the scope beneath the question. For this dossier, compare only the opening incidents and their immediate consequences. Do not invent later chapters or explain the characters' complete histories.

Step 2: Build the Evidence Ledger

Start with separate ledgers. Keep exact details in the first column and interpretation in later columns.

Dimension Passage A: Orchard Register Passage B: Bell House
Character pressure Ivo must challenge a registrar's account before the scarf and soil disappear as evidence. Sela must decide whether recording the bells protects knowledge or exposes her to the village rule.
Narrative access An outside narrator gives Ivo's discoveries and memory; the registrar's withheld log remains inaccessible. The scene follows Sela's hearing and notebook, but the village head and neighbor offer competing explanations.
Story-world chronology 07:10 scarf; 06:40 ticket discovered at noon; evening soil line. The order is reconstructed after the fact. 05:00 double ring; three days later single ring; end of week third ring. Repetition creates a developing rule.
Telling order Scarf and official account first, ticket later, soil last. Delayed evidence revises “voluntary departure.” Double ring, official dismissal, notebook, later ring, then Sela's own door. The pattern escalates from public event to personal threat.
Recurring detail “Closed” and the green scarf move from notice and object to traces of contested access. Bell rings and names recur with changing counts; sound becomes a social record.
Genre contract Mystery or bureaucratic realism: records, dates, and material traces invite investigation. Gothic or speculative mystery: an uncanny rule invites testing, but social silence may explain the bell.
World rule and omission The registry can classify departure, but the log is withheld; we do not know who removed the scarf. Bell counts are treated as a village rule, but its mechanism and origin are unknown.
Immediate consequence Ivo's search moves from the road to the archive and implicates the institution. Sela's act of recording becomes risky when the bell reaches her own door.

The ledger is not a collection of decorations. Each row should answer: what changes in the character's available action or knowledge because of this formal choice?

Step 3: Separate Claim Levels

For every important observation, label the level of certainty.

Established observation

Passage A includes a bus receipt dated 06:40, while the registrar says Mara left at dawn and refuses the departure log.

Strong inference

The official account is under pressure, and the archive may be participating in the disappearance rather than neutrally recording it.

Open question

We do not know whether Mara chose to leave, whether someone staged the receipt, or why the soil line ends at the archive.

Unsupported speculation

The registrar is definitely a supernatural creature who transformed Mara.

Make the same distinctions for Passage B. The double and third rings support an inference that the village rule is changing or being enforced against Sela. They do not prove that the bell has independent consciousness.

This separation protects the dossier from two failures: plot summary that never makes a claim, and confident symbolism that outruns the passage.

Step 4: Write Passage-Level Claims Before Comparing

Write one paragraph for each passage before writing the comparison. Use the chain from lesson 007:

observation -> mechanism -> effect -> limit

Model for Passage A

The passage turns absence into a conflict between material traces and institutional records. The scarf, dated receipt, and soil line give Ivo evidence that does not fit the registrar's “voluntary departure,” while the withheld log prevents him from testing the official timeline. The delayed evidence changes the genre contract from a simple departure report to a bureaucratic mystery. The passage suggests that the archive helps shape what can count as truth, but it leaves Mara's actual choice unresolved.

Model for Passage B

The passage turns absence into a public sound rule whose meaning changes as the bell count changes. Sela's notebook gives her a private record, but the neighbor's statement shows that naming and physical presence can be separated. The final ring at Sela's door moves the world rule from a story about her father to a direct threat against her. The bell may be supernatural, social, or both; the passage establishes the pressure of the rule more firmly than its origin.

These paragraphs do not need identical vocabulary. Comparison is strongest when each passage is allowed to have its own mechanism first.

Step 5: Build the Comparative Claim

Use a three-part structure:

  1. Shared problem: What question do both passages make urgent?
  2. Formal difference: Which choices make their treatments different?
  3. Consequence: How does that difference change knowledge, agency, sympathy, or uncertainty?

A defensible thesis might be:

Both passages make absence a struggle over public knowledge, but the Orchard Register locates that struggle in delayed documents and institutional classification, while Bell House locates it in a repeated sound whose social meaning escalates. As a result, Ivo's agency depends on recovering material evidence from an archive, whereas Sela's agency becomes entangled with whether recording a communal rule will make her its next subject. Neither passage resolves whether the system is intentionally coercive, so the comparison should claim unequal access and pressure rather than a single hidden cause.

Notice the verbs: “locates,” “escalates,” and “becomes entangled” describe formal effects. The thesis does not say that one passage is more realistic or that both symbols mean the same thing.

Step 6: Add a Counterreading and Revision Path

An honest dossier tests itself. Write a plausible alternative:

Perhaps Passage A is not primarily about institutional coercion. The soil line may be a literal trail, and the registrar may be protecting privacy rather than hiding a crime. Perhaps Passage B's bell is only a community ritual, and Sela's third ring reflects her fear rather than a changed rule.

Then answer three questions:

  • Which details does the counterreading explain well?
  • Which details does it leave awkward or unexplained?
  • What future evidence would make you revise the primary claim?

For the primary comparison, the withheld log and escalating bell count remain difficult to treat as neutral background. A later scene showing an independent archive audit or another family hearing the same sequence could revise the claims about intention and mechanism. The uncertainty is part of the result, not a missing final paragraph.

Step 7: Assemble the Dossier

Use this compact structure for your final artifact:

  1. Question and scope — one shared question, texts or passages, and boundaries.
  2. Passage A ledger — observations, timeline, access, pattern, genre, world rule.
  3. Passage B ledger — the same categories, without forcing identical evidence.
  4. Passage-level claims — one bounded paragraph per passage.
  5. Comparative thesis — shared problem, formal difference, consequence.
  6. Counterreading — a credible alternative and what it explains.
  7. Uncertainty and revision path — what remains open and what evidence would change your mind.
  8. Self-review — score the artifact against the rubric below.

Capstone Rubric

Criterion Strong Developing Missing
Evidence Uses exact details and distinguishes observation from inference. Uses some details but merges them with assumptions. Relies on plot summary or personal reaction.
Formal analysis Explains access, structure, pattern, genre, and world rules as mechanisms. Names tools without tracing their effects. Treats form as decoration or vocabulary recall.
Comparison Shows a shared question and a meaningful formal difference. Lists similarities and differences without consequence. Compares themes only or discusses one passage.
Calibration Includes counterreading, uncertainty, and revision evidence. Mentions ambiguity without testing the claim. Presents speculation as fact.
Artifact quality Tables and paragraphs let another reader audit the reasoning. Notes are usable but uneven or repetitive. No clear structure or evidence trail.

Aim for “Strong” on every criterion before calling the dossier complete.

Self-Review Checklist

Before submitting, ask:

  • Did I state one question that both passages can answer?
  • Did I cite exact words, actions, timings, or omissions rather than only themes?
  • Did I reconstruct telling order when it changes the first interpretation?
  • Did I say who can know what and which account is under pressure?
  • Did I test a motif across recurrence and variation rather than use a symbol dictionary?
  • Did I identify the genre contract and any deliberate violation?
  • Did I distinguish world facts, strong inferences, open questions, and invented lore?
  • Did I explain how a formal difference changes agency, sympathy, or uncertainty?
  • Did I include a counterreading that could genuinely challenge my claim?
  • Did I state what evidence would make me revise it?

If any answer is no, revise the ledger before polishing the prose.

Trade-offs and Limits

The central trade-off is coverage versus attention. A dossier can make connections visible, but too many categories can turn reading into bookkeeping. Use the smallest evidence set that proves the comparison. A table is a scaffold, not a substitute for encountering the language of each passage.

Comparison also risks false equivalence. Two texts can ask the same question while distributing evidence, agency, and ambiguity in completely different ways. The goal is not to force symmetry; it is to make the difference answerable.

The capstone has a final boundary: a well-supported dossier is not a definitive decoding of a text. It is a transparent, revisable interpretation that another reader can inspect, challenge, and extend.

Key Takeaways

  • A comparative dossier asks one shared question and lets each passage answer through its own formal mechanisms.
  • Keep observation, inference, open question, and unsupported speculation visibly separate.
  • Compare consequences for knowledge, agency, sympathy, and uncertainty—not themes alone.
  • A counterreading and revision path make interpretation auditable rather than merely confident.
  • The goal is a precise, revisable encounter with texts, not a complete decoding or taxonomy.
