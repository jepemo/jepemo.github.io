Delegation, Impersonation, and Admin Actions

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • distinguish acting for a user, impersonating a principal, and exercising a privileged admin role;

  • trace the identity chain, scope, expiry, and reason behind an elevated action;

  • design a support or break-glass action that remains reviewable after the incident.

Idea in one sentence: Privileged access is defensible only when the system preserves who initiated it, who or what it acts as, why it was allowed, what it can do, and when it ends.

Core Insight

At 02:14, Northstar support engineer Lena investigates why a customer's export failed. The customer authorizes support to view its export configuration. Lena needs a temporary read-only view of the Acme tenant; she must not silently become the customer administrator, create a new API key, or keep the access after the incident. The investigation succeeds only if later reviewers can distinguish Lena's action from the customer's ordinary actions.

“Admin mode” is not an authorization design. It erases the facts that make privileged access safe. A delegated action means an original principal is allowed to act for another principal or within a granted scope. Impersonation means the system uses an effective identity's permissions, such as a service account or customer user, while preserving the original actor. A privileged admin action is an explicitly authorized operation performed by an operator under an elevated role. These can overlap, but their audit and consent requirements differ.

The useful identity chain is:

actor: lena@northstar.example
effective subject: support-readonly
target tenant: acme-eu
action: exports.configuration.read
grant: incident INC-4812, customer-approved
expiry: 03:14 UTC

The trade-off is operational support versus abuse potential. Broad permanent support roles make incidents easier in the moment but create a standing path to customer data. Time-bound, scoped grants add approval, logging, and workflow cost; that cost is what preserves accountability.

Keep Actor and Effective Subject Separate

An authorization request needs more than one identity when delegation exists. The actor is the authenticated principal that initiated the request. The effective subject is the identity whose permissions are being evaluated. A support session might evaluate a restricted support role; a service may impersonate a workload identity; a user may delegate one action to an assistant. Never replace the actor with the effective subject in logs or downstream calls.

For Lena, a safe grant is narrow:

actor allowed: lena@northstar.example
effective role: support-readonly
tenant: acme-eu
resources: export configuration and failed-job metadata
operations: read only
reason: INC-4812
approval: customer contact + on-call lead
expires_at: 03:14 UTC

The policy must check both sides: may Lena activate support-readonly, and may that role read these Acme resources for this incident? The tenant boundary from the previous lesson still applies. A support grant does not mean tenant=*, and it does not make customer secrets, credentials, or billing data visible unless those fields are explicitly in scope.

Pause and predict: If the audit record says only customer-admin changed export configuration, can an investigator prove whether the customer or Lena made the change?

Check: No. The effective identity alone loses the initiator. Record actor, effective subject, target tenant, delegation or grant ID, reason, approval, policy version, and result for every delegated request.

Worked Path: A Time-Bound Support Session

Lena opens the support workflow rather than a generic admin console. It requires MFA, incident INC-4812, Acme tenant selection, a reason, and approval. The authorization service creates a short-lived support session rather than adding Lena to a permanent group.

1. Lena authenticates with MFA and requests support-readonly for acme-eu.
2. Workflow verifies her eligible support role, incident state, customer approval, and on-call approval.
3. Service issues a short-lived session with actor=Lena and effective_role=support-readonly.
4. Lena requests failed export job J-771.
5. Enforcement checks session expiry, tenant=acme-eu, action=jobs.read, and resource ownership.
6. Policy allows the read and records the decision chain.
7. At expiry or revocation, the session cannot create new requests; active streams are handled by explicit product policy.

This is just-in-time access: the privilege exists only when activated for a bounded purpose. It is safer than a shared support credential because it is attributable, can require current authentication, and has an automatic end. It is not automatically safe: a session can still be too broad, an approver can be compromised, or the enforcement point can be bypassed.

Impersonating a service account has the same need for a chain. A deployment system may obtain a short-lived token for scheduled-exporter so it can test that workload's access. The authorization record should identify the human or workload that requested the impersonation and the service account whose permissions were used. Long-lived service-account keys lose that provenance because anyone holding the key appears only as the service account.

Pause and predict: An urgent incident needs production access but the approval service is unavailable. What is safer: a permanent shared emergency password or a break-glass flow that creates a short-lived, heavily audited grant?

Check: Use the controlled break-glass path if one exists. It should require stronger authentication, a narrow scope, expiry, immediate alerting, and post-incident review. Emergency access is an exception with more evidence, not an excuse to remove it.

So far: delegation changes the authorization context, not the history of who acted. Scope, expiry, and evidence must travel with the request and be enforced at every protected operation.

Scope, Consent, and Revocation

The smallest useful grant names a tenant, resources, actions, duration, and reason. “Help customer” is not a scope. “Read export configuration and job J-771 in Acme until 03:14 for INC-4812” is a scope an enforcement point can evaluate.

Consent may be customer approval, an internal approver, a delegated authority established earlier, or a documented emergency rule. The system should distinguish these cases. Customer consent does not authorize a platform engineer to edit billing. A manager's approval for an emergency role does not authorize a user to impersonate another user indefinitely. Use separate actions and policies for read support, configuration change, credential recovery, and destructive repair.

Revocation must be designed, not assumed. Expiry prevents future requests after a deadline. Explicit revocation ends a grant early when the incident closes or an approval is withdrawn. Sessions, caches, tokens, and background jobs need a freshness rule: a revoked grant must not remain usable solely because an old decision was cached. For high-impact actions, recheck the grant at the operation boundary and deny if the grant state is unknown.

Destructive actions deserve a second confirmation boundary. Reading a failed export configuration may use one approved support session; rotating a customer key, deleting data, or changing a tenant administrator should require a more specific action grant, an explicit target, and often a second approver. Do not assume that an elevated session makes every next click equally safe. The policy should distinguish exports.configuration.read from keys.rotate, and the user interface should make the effective subject and tenant visible before the action is confirmed.

The session lifecycle also needs a clean end. Closing the browser is not a reliable revocation event, and an expired display timer is not enforcement. The server must reject new requests after expiry, workers must avoid continuing queued privileged work without a valid grant, and renewal should require another policy decision rather than silently extending access. A post-incident review can then compare the requested scope, the actions actually taken, and the remaining access paths to confirm that the emergency grant was removed.

Delegation chains should have a depth limit and an understandable rule. If Lena delegates to a tool which delegates to another service, a later enforcement point must not lose the original actor or accept an unlimited chain of inherited authority. Preserve the chain, constrain each step to no more authority than its parent grant, and refuse a chain the system cannot explain. This prevents an apparently narrow support session from becoming a general privilege-escalation mechanism.

Failure Modes and Evidence

Shared credentials. A team shares a powerful password or API key. The service sees one identity and cannot establish who used it. Replace shared secrets with attributable, short-lived delegation or impersonation tokens.

Role inheritance by accident. A support session is treated as customer admin and inherits create, delete, or credential actions. Define a separate support role and test every sensitive action as denied.

Missing tenant or reason. A valid support role becomes an unbounded cross-tenant search tool. Require tenant and reason in the grant and in audit evidence; reject a request whose context is incomplete.

Approval without enforcement. A ticket is approved but the API never checks the grant. Make the grant an authorization input, not a human process beside the system.

Break-glass without review. Emergency access is used but no alert, expiry, or retrospective review occurs. Alert on activation, record every action, and require a time-bound incident follow-up.

Useful evidence includes the actor, effective subject, grant ID, approval identity, tenant, request ID, action, resource scope, policy version, expiry, decision, and reason. Redact credentials and customer content. Review activations that exceed expected duration, cross tenant boundaries, attempt denied actions, or produce destructive changes.

Correlate those records with the incident timeline so a reviewer can tell whether access was activated before the customer approved it, used after the incident ended, or renewed without a new reason.

This correlation also exposes actions attempted outside the approved operational scope.

Design Check

Start with this case: an on-call engineer must restart Acme's stuck export worker but must not read invoice contents or administer Zephyr.

actor: on-call engineer
effective role: export-operations
tenant: acme-eu
action: workers.restart for export worker only
reason and approval: incident ID + on-call record
expiry: 30 minutes
denied: invoice.read, credentials.read, any zephyr action
evidence: actor/effective-role/grant/tenant/action/result

Now design one delegated or admin action. Name the original actor, effective subject, tenant, exact operation, approval or consent, expiry, revocation behavior, and the audit fields that prove the chain. Accept the design only if a reviewer can answer who acted, for whom, why, what they could do, and when access ended.

Key Takeaways

  • Preserve both the original actor and the effective subject; impersonation must not erase accountability.
  • A privileged grant needs tenant, action, resource scope, reason, approval, expiry, revocation behavior, and enforcement at the operation boundary.
  • Just-in-time and break-glass access reduce standing privilege only when they are short-lived, narrowly scoped, alerted, and reviewed.
  • Evidence must explain the delegation chain without storing credentials or unnecessary customer data.
