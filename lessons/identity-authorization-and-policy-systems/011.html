RBAC, Groups, Roles, and Permission Drift

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • separate groups, roles, permissions, and bindings in an authorization decision;

  • trace a request to its effective permissions and identify a stale grant;

  • design a reviewable RBAC change without turning one exception into a permanent broad role.

Idea in one sentence: RBAC makes access manageable by granting reusable job-function roles, but it stays safe only when memberships, bindings, and exceptions are reviewed as the organization changes.

Core Insight

Maya works in billing support for a SaaS company. Her normal job is to read invoices and open refund requests. During a holiday incident, the finance lead adds her to a temporary refund-escalation group so she can approve a few stuck refunds. The incident ends, but the group membership remains. Six months later, Maya has moved to a different support team and can still approve refunds because the old assignment continues to grant a role nobody intended her to keep.

The authorization check may be working exactly as configured. Maya authenticated successfully, the directory still reports the group, the group is bound to an approval role, and that role contains the approval permission. The failure is not necessarily a broken if statement. The system's effective permissions no longer match the permissions Maya's current job needs. That mismatch is permission drift.

Role-based access control, or RBAC, manages access through roles that represent relatively stable responsibilities such as billing support, refund approver, or finance auditor. In plain language, a role is a reusable bundle of allowed actions. Instead of granting every action separately to every person, the system gives the role its permissions and assigns people to the role, often through groups.

A common mistake is to treat a group and a role as the same thing. A group answers “which principals are collected together?” A role answers “which permissions does this responsibility carry?” Keeping those separate makes membership changes, temporary exceptions, and reviews visible. The trade-off is administrative simplicity versus overbroad access: fewer, broader roles are easy to manage, but they make it easier for old or unrelated permissions to survive unnoticed.

The Pieces That Produce an Allow Decision

RBAC starts with a small model. A principal is the actor asking for access: a human user, service account, or workload identity. A permission is an allowed action on a resource or resource family, such as invoices.read or refunds.approve. A role is a named, reusable set of permissions. A group is a set of principals, usually managed by a directory or identity provider. A binding connects a principal or group to a role, sometimes within a scope such as a tenant, project, or namespace.

For Maya, the intended arrangement might be:

principal: maya@northstar.example

group: billing-support-emea
  -> binding: billing-support role in the EMEA support workspace

role: billing-support
  -> permissions: invoices.read, refunds.request, customers.note.write

role: refund-approver
  -> permissions: refunds.approve

The important path is not the labels themselves. It is the relationships:

principal -> group memberships -> role bindings -> roles -> permissions -> decision

In the core RBAC model, users are assigned to roles and permissions are assigned to roles. Groups are a practical way to manage many user-to-role assignments without editing a role every time someone joins or leaves a team. A Kubernetes RoleBinding shows the same separation clearly: it names subjects, references a role, and limits where that grant takes effect. The binding is the grant; the group is not a bag of unexplained permission names.

This distinction matters when changing access. Moving Maya from billing support to sales should normally remove her from billing-support-emea. It should not require editing the billing-support role, because other support staff still need that role. Conversely, changing the role's permissions affects everyone bound to it, so it needs a different level of review.

Pause and predict: A manager asks for a one-week refund exception for Maya. Should the team add refunds.approve to the permanent billing-support role?

Check: No. That would grant approval to every billing-support member and preserve it beyond the exception. Use a separately named, narrowly scoped temporary binding or role, with an owner and an expiry that can be reviewed and removed.

Worked Path: How a Temporary Exception Becomes Drift

At first, Maya's ordinary refund request follows the intended path. She signs in, the identity system verifies her session, and the authorization service resolves her group memberships. It finds billing-support-emea, follows its binding to billing-support, and expands that role into the permissions needed for her normal work. Maya can read an invoice and submit a refund request, but she cannot approve the final refund.

During the holiday incident, the finance lead needs an approver available in EMEA hours. The safe change is explicit:

temporary group: refund-escalation-2025
member: maya@northstar.example
binding: refund-escalation-2025 -> refund-approver
owner: finance-operations
expiry: 2025-12-31
reason: holiday refund backlog

Now trace a later request:

request:
  principal: maya@northstar.example
  action: refunds.approve
  resource: refund R-774

directory result:
  billing-support-emea
  refund-escalation-2025

effective roles:
  billing-support
  refund-approver

effective permissions:
  invoices.read
  refunds.request
  customers.note.write
  refunds.approve

decision:
  allow, because refund-approver contains refunds.approve

The authorization service does not know that the holiday incident ended. It sees current membership and a current binding. If the expiry is not enforced, or if nobody reviews the group before it expires, the temporary exception has become a durable privilege. The mechanical decision is correct; the administrative state is wrong.

This is why an access review must inspect effective access, not only role definitions. Looking at billing-support alone would show a reasonable role. Looking at Maya's user profile alone might show two innocent-looking groups. The risk appears only when the system expands both groups through their bindings and roles.

Pause and predict: If Maya is removed from billing-support-emea but remains in refund-escalation-2025, can she still approve refunds?

Check: Yes. Removing the ordinary support membership removes the support role, but the separate exception group still resolves to refund-approver. To revoke the approval, remove or expire that exception binding or membership and verify the new effective permissions.

So far: roles make a reusable permission bundle; groups make assignments manageable; bindings connect the two. Drift occurs when one of those relationships survives after its business reason has disappeared.

Permission Drift Is a Lifecycle Problem

Permission drift is the gap between the permissions a principal can currently exercise and the permissions justified by their present responsibility. It is not limited to a bad termination process. A person may stay employed, a service may remain healthy, and every policy check may return a valid answer while the authorization model gradually becomes too broad.

Common sources of drift include:

  • Joiner, mover, leaver changes. A new hire receives the right groups; a team transfer removes only some of them; an offboarding event disables the account but leaves a service identity or delegated access path active.
  • Temporary access without a lifecycle. An incident, migration, or audit exception is implemented as a direct grant or a special group with no owner, expiry, ticket, or removal check.
  • Role growth. Someone adds one powerful permission to a convenient existing role because creating a new role feels expensive. Every current and future assignee receives the new power.
  • Indirect membership. Nested groups, role hierarchies, synchronized directory groups, and project-wide bindings make the grant transitive. An administrator reviews the direct assignment and misses the inherited one.
  • Scope that is too broad. A role intended for one tenant, namespace, or project is bound at organization or cluster scope. The permission itself may be reasonable; the resource boundary is not.

The goal is not to eliminate every exception. A business may genuinely need emergency approval or temporary migration access. The control is to make the exception legible: who approved it, what it grants, where it applies, when it expires, and how the system proves it was removed. A direct individual grant is sometimes appropriate for a break-glass path, but it should be more visible and more short-lived than a normal role assignment.

RBAC also has a limit. It works best when the permission maps mainly to a stable function. “Billing support may open refund requests” is a role-shaped rule. “Allow only when this user owns the document, belongs to the resource's tenant, is on-call, and the record is not under legal hold” depends on attributes and relationships. The next lesson introduces ABAC and ReBAC for those cases; do not hide that complexity inside a misleadingly broad RBAC role.

Designing Roles That Can Be Reviewed

Good RBAC design starts with the work, not with a list of people. Identify a repeatable responsibility, the smallest actions it needs, the resource scope, and who owns the role. Name the role for that responsibility, such as billing-refund-approver, rather than for the first person or incident that needed it.

Then separate three changes that are often confused:

membership change: Maya joins or leaves a group
binding change: a group receives or loses a role in a scope
role change: the role gains or loses a permission

Each change has a different blast radius. Membership changes usually affect one principal. A binding changes access for every current group member in its scope. A role change can affect every principal bound to the role, including indirect or future members. Reviewing them as if they were equally small is how a one-person request becomes an organization-wide permission change.

Use least privilege, but avoid role explosion. A separate role for every employee is merely a renamed access-control list and is hard to review. One global support-admin role is simple but broad. The useful middle ground is a small set of roles aligned to stable duties, narrow scopes where the platform supports them, and a documented process for exceptional access.

Separation of duties belongs here too. If one person can create a vendor, approve that vendor's payment, and alter the audit trail, the problem is not only a broad permission. It is a workflow that has no independent check. RBAC can express separate roles such as requester and approver, but the application must still prevent a person from approving their own request when that business constraint applies.

Evidence and Failure Modes

When an authorization decision matters, an operator should be able to reconstruct it without guessing. A useful allow or deny record includes the principal, requested action and resource scope, resolved groups, matched bindings, effective roles, policy version, decision, correlation ID, and the reason for a denial. Do not log raw tokens, passwords, or more personal data than the investigation needs.

Those records support both incident response and periodic access review. A review can ask: Which high-privilege roles have no owner? Which temporary bindings are past their expiry? Which principals have direct grants? Which roles have acquired permissions since the last review? Which group-to-role bindings now apply across a wider scope than intended? The answer should come from an effective-permission report, not a spreadsheet someone assembled from memory.

Watch for these failure modes:

  • The convenience role: a generic admin or support role accumulates unrelated powers until no team can describe its true contract.
  • The invisible exception: a direct grant or temporary group has no expiry, owner, or review evidence.
  • The hierarchy surprise: a nested group or inherited role grants a sensitive permission through a path reviewers did not inspect.
  • The stale cache: a group removal is correct in the directory but an authorization cache continues to allow it. Set clear freshness expectations, invalidate or bound the cache, and test revocation behavior.

The trade-off is deliberate. Central roles and groups reduce the cost of adding or moving people, while expiry, ownership, reviews, and effective-access reports add operational work. That work is what keeps administrative convenience from becoming quiet privilege accumulation.

Design Check

Start with this nearby case. A contractor needs to read invoices for the acme-eu support workspace for 30 days. They must not request or approve refunds.

group: acme-eu-invoice-contractors
binding: acme-eu-invoice-contractors -> invoice-reader in acme-eu
role: invoice-reader
permissions: invoices.read
owner: support-operations
expiry: contract end date

This is reviewable because the duty, scope, owner, and expiry are explicit. It does not add a contractor to a broad support role or change the role used by permanent employees.

Now choose one real access change and write:

principal or group:
role:
permissions and scope:
binding:
owner and expiry or review cadence:
effective-permission check after the change:
failure if the grant remains after its reason ends:

Use this rubric before accepting the design: the role must name a stable responsibility; the permission must be narrower than a convenience admin role; the binding must make its scope visible; and a reviewer must be able to tell why, until when, and for whom the grant exists.

Key Takeaways

  • A group collects principals, a role collects permissions, and a binding grants that role to the group or principal in a scope.
  • Effective permissions come from the whole assignment path, so access reviews must inspect memberships, bindings, role contents, and inheritance together.
  • Permission drift is a lifecycle failure: temporary access, old memberships, broad role edits, and indirect grants can outlive their business reason.
  • Use roles for stable responsibilities; make exceptional access owned, narrow, time-bound, and easy to prove removed.
