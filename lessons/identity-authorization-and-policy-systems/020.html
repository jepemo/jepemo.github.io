SSO, SAML, and Enterprise Federation

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • trace a SAML sign-in from service-provider request to local session;

  • identify which trust decisions belong to the IdP, the service provider, and local authorization;

  • review certificate rotation, attribute mapping, and IdP-initiated login for failure modes.

Idea in one sentence: Enterprise federation lets an external identity provider authenticate users for your application, trading customer convenience for precise trust configuration, assertion validation, and local authorization boundaries.

Core Insight

Acme Corp uses your SaaS product for invoices. Their employees do not want another password; they expect to click the invoice app from their company portal and arrive signed in. Acme's identity provider, not your app, authenticates the employee with company MFA and device policy. Your app, acting as a service provider, accepts a signed SAML assertion and creates a local session.

Now Acme rotates its SAML signing certificate on Friday night. Their identity provider starts signing assertions with the new certificate. Your service provider still trusts only the old one. On Monday, every Acme user fails login. A rushed support fix adds the new certificate, but if the system accepts any certificate in a pasted metadata file without checking issuer, audience, and tenant binding, the outage response can become a trust-boundary bug.

Single sign-on is not just a convenience feature. It is a delegated authentication contract. The customer says, "trust this identity provider to authenticate these users for this tenant." Your application must answer a narrower question: "Does this exact assertion, from this exact issuer, for this exact audience, at this time, map to the intended local account and tenant?" Only after that can local authorization decide what the user may do.

SAML, the Security Assertion Markup Language, is one common protocol for that contract. The protocol carries signed XML assertions with statements about a subject and attributes. Those assertions can be powerful, but they are not magic proof of access. The service provider must validate signature, issuer, audience, recipient, timestamps, replay identifiers, tenant configuration, and attribute mapping before it creates a session.

The trade-off is customer convenience versus configuration and trust complexity. Federation removes a local password database for enterprise users and lets customers enforce their own authentication policy. In return, your app must operate a high-stakes configuration surface: metadata, certificates, entity IDs, ACS URLs, NameID formats, group or role attributes, JIT provisioning, and troubleshooting evidence.

What Moves During SAML Login?

Start with the visible pieces. In the Acme scenario:

identity provider (IdP):
  issuer/entityID: https://idp.acme.example/saml
  signing certificate: acme-cert-2026
  authenticates: Priya with Acme MFA
  sends: signed assertion

service provider (SP):
  entityID/audience: https://invoices.example/saml/acme
  ACS URL: https://invoices.example/saml/acs/acme
  trusted certificate: acme-cert-2026
  creates: local session for tenant acme-eu

local app:
  maps subject to user
  applies tenant policy
  assigns local roles from groups or mappings
  records login evidence

The service provider usually starts an SP-initiated flow by redirecting the browser to the IdP with a SAML authentication request. The IdP authenticates the user and posts a signed SAML response back to the SP's assertion consumer service, often called the ACS endpoint. The SP validates the response and creates a local application session.

IdP-initiated login starts from the customer's portal. The browser arrives at the ACS endpoint with a SAML response but without a request created by the SP moments earlier. That can be useful for customer portals, but it removes some request correlation. The SP must rely even more carefully on tenant routing, issuer, audience, recipient, timestamps, and replay protection.

The central distinction is this: the IdP authenticates; the SP validates and binds; the application authorizes. If Priya signs in through Acme, the assertion may tell your app her subject identifier, email, display name, and groups. It does not automatically prove she may approve invoices, export data, or act in another tenant. Those are local decisions that use the assertion as one input.

Worked Path: Validating Acme's Assertion

Follow Priya's SP-initiated login.

1. Priya opens invoices.example/acme.
2. SP finds tenant acme-eu and builds AuthnRequest request_481.
3. Browser redirects to Acme IdP with request_481.
4. IdP authenticates Priya and signs a SAML response.
5. Browser POSTs response to /saml/acs/acme.
6. SP selects Acme's SAML configuration from the tenant route.
7. SP validates issuer, signature, certificate, audience, recipient, and time bounds.
8. SP checks InResponseTo=request_481 and rejects replayed assertion IDs.
9. SP maps subject and attributes to a local user link.
10. App creates a local session and evaluates local authorization.
11. Login evidence records tenant, issuer, cert fingerprint, subject, and result.

The dangerous mistake is to treat step 5 as login complete. Receiving a SAML response means only that the browser delivered a blob of XML to the ACS endpoint. The SP still has to prove that the blob came from the configured IdP, was intended for this service provider, is current, has not been replayed, and belongs to the tenant being accessed.

Signature validation proves the assertion was signed by a trusted private key. Issuer validation proves the signed assertion claims to come from the IdP configured for this tenant. Audience validation proves the assertion was meant for this service provider, not another app. Recipient and ACS validation prove it was delivered to the expected endpoint. Time bounds limit how long the assertion can be used. Replay checks stop a captured assertion from being submitted again.

bad shortcut:
  signed by any known customer cert -> create session

safer binding:
  tenant route -> exact IdP config
  exact issuer -> exact trusted certificate
  exact audience -> this SP entityID
  exact recipient -> this ACS URL
  fresh assertion -> no replayed assertion ID

Pause and predict: Why is a valid signature not enough to accept a SAML assertion?

Check: A signature only says a trusted key signed the assertion. The SP still needs to know which tenant that key belongs to, whether the assertion was issued by the configured IdP, whether the audience and recipient match this app, and whether the assertion is fresh.

Configuration Is Part of the Security Boundary

SAML security often fails in configuration, not in the idea of federation itself. The metadata file, certificate set, entity IDs, ACS URLs, NameID format, and attribute mappings are security-sensitive state.

Certificate rotation is the most common operational pressure. Customers rotate signing keys for good reasons: expiry, incident response, or standard key hygiene. A mature SP supports multiple active certificates during a planned rollover, records certificate fingerprints in login evidence, warns before expiry, and makes it clear which certificate signed recent assertions. A brittle SP treats rotation as an emergency paste operation and cannot explain which key was trusted when.

Attribute mapping is another boundary. The assertion may contain email, NameID, groups, department, or custom claims. Your application needs stable rules for linking the assertion subject to a local user. Email can change; group display names can drift. The previous SCIM lesson matters here: SAML attributes may help just-in-time account creation, but SCIM is usually the better source for durable lifecycle state. Do not let a login assertion silently recreate a disabled account because an email matched.

JIT provisioning, or just-in-time provisioning, creates a local user when the first successful federated login arrives. It is convenient during onboarding, but it should be narrow. JIT can create an identity link and maybe a default low-risk membership. High-impact roles should come from explicit group mapping, SCIM provisioning, or local admin configuration. Otherwise a malformed or overly broad group attribute can become authorization.

IdP-initiated flows need extra care because the SP did not create an authentication request. If your app supports them, route the response to one tenant configuration before validation, require exact issuer and audience matching, reject ambiguous account discovery, and record that the flow was IdP-initiated. Many enterprise customers need the feature; the design should make the weaker correlation visible rather than pretending it is equivalent to SP-initiated login.

Failure Modes and Operational Evidence

When SSO breaks, customers experience it as total product outage. When SSO is misvalidated, the risk is unauthorized access. The operational design needs to support both fast diagnosis and conservative trust behavior.

Common failures include:

  • Certificate mismatch: the IdP rotates signing keys and the SP still trusts only the old certificate.
  • Issuer confusion: two tenants use similar metadata and the SP accepts an assertion under the wrong tenant.
  • Audience omission: an assertion intended for another application is accepted by this one.
  • Recipient or ACS mismatch: a response is posted to an endpoint that was not the intended recipient.
  • Replay weakness: the same assertion can be submitted twice within its time window.
  • Unsafe JIT: a successful login creates or reactivates an account that local lifecycle policy should keep disabled.
  • Group overtrust: raw SAML group names become admin roles without stable mapping and review.

Good login evidence answers a small set of questions without exposing secrets: which tenant configuration was used, which issuer was accepted, which certificate fingerprint validated the signature, which audience and recipient were checked, which subject was mapped, which attributes were consumed, which flow type was used, and why a response was rejected. This is not optional observability. It is the only way support can distinguish "customer rotated a cert" from "assertion was for the wrong audience" from "local authorization denied after login succeeded."

Pause and predict: A user signs in successfully but has no invoice-admin permissions even though the SAML assertion contains Finance Admins. Did SSO fail?

Check: Not necessarily. SSO may have authenticated the user correctly. Local authorization may require a stable group mapping, SCIM-provisioned role, tenant assignment, or reviewed admin grant before granting invoice-admin access.

Design Check

Review a SAML integration by walking one login, one rotation, and one misconfiguration.

Login:
  Which tenant configuration is selected before validation?
  Are issuer, signature, audience, recipient, timestamps, and replay checked?
  How is the assertion subject linked to a local user?

Authorization:
  Which attributes are only profile data?
  Which attributes can influence local roles?
  What prevents SSO from reactivating a deprovisioned user?

Rotation:
  Can old and new certificates overlap safely?
  Are expiry, fingerprint, and recent usage visible to support?

Misconfiguration:
  What evidence explains a rejected assertion?
  Does the system fail closed when tenant, audience, or certificate is ambiguous?

Apply the check to Acme. A strong design has a per-tenant SAML config, accepts only Acme's issuer and active certs for Acme, validates audience against the Acme SP entity ID, rejects replayed assertion IDs, maps NameID to a stable local identity link, and treats groups as inputs to reviewed local role mappings. It lets Acme stage a new certificate before cutover and logs which fingerprint signed each successful login.

A weak design stores all customer certificates in one global trust pool, picks a tenant after reading the email address, accepts IdP-initiated responses without audience checks, and turns any group named Admin into local admin. That design may appear convenient during onboarding, but it has no crisp trust boundary when something goes wrong.

Close the lesson and reconstruct the path from memory: tenant selection, AuthnRequest, IdP authentication, signed response, assertion validation, subject mapping, session creation, local authorization, login evidence. If you cannot place a validation step before session creation, that is the part of the federation boundary to inspect.

Connections

SCIM keeps local identity and group state aligned over time. SSO and SAML handle login-time authentication. They often work together, but they answer different questions: "who can authenticate right now?" versus "what local lifecycle and authorization state should this app use?"

Key Takeaways

  • SSO delegates authentication to an enterprise IdP; it does not eliminate local account linking, lifecycle state, or authorization.
  • A SAML response must be bound to the exact tenant, issuer, certificate, audience, recipient, time window, and replay state before a local session is created.
  • Federation configuration is security-sensitive state: certificate rotation, metadata, ACS URLs, NameID, and attribute mapping all need review and evidence.
  • Treat SAML attributes as inputs, not automatic permissions; local policy decides what roles and data access the authenticated user receives.
