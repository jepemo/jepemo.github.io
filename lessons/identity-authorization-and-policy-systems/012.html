ABAC, ReBAC, and Relationship-Based Authorization

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • identify whether an access rule needs a stable role, attributes, relationships, or a combination;

  • trace an authorization decision across subject, resource, and contextual facts;

  • name the source, freshness requirement, and failure behavior for facts that influence access.

Idea in one sentence: Use attributes for facts about the request and use relationships for facts about how people, groups, and resources are connected; both need trustworthy, current evidence before they can grant access.

Core Insight

Northstar's product team shares a rollout plan with an external delivery partner. Nina is a contractor, not a Northstar employee. She should be able to view documents in the atlas project while her engagement is active, but not documents marked restricted, documents under legal hold, or anything after her sponsor revokes the relationship. The project is also nested in folders, and some access is inherited from the project rather than written on every document.

A simple project-viewer role can express part of that rule. It says that project viewers may read project documents. It cannot by itself say whether this specific person is still an active contractor, whether this specific document is restricted, whether Nina is connected to this project through the partner team, or whether her device satisfies the current access requirement. Creating a separate role for every partner, project, classification, and device state would turn RBAC into an unreviewable list of near-duplicate roles.

Attribute-based access control, or ABAC, evaluates named facts about the subject, resource, requested action, and sometimes the environment. In this example, contractor_active, device_managed, document_classification, and request_risk are attributes. In plain language, ABAC asks, “given the facts true right now, does this request meet the rule?”

Relationship-based access control, or ReBAC, evaluates how entities are connected. “Nina is a member of the delivery partner team,” “the delivery partner team is a viewer of project Atlas,” and “this document belongs to project Atlas” are relationships. In plain language, ReBAC asks, “is there an allowed path from this principal to this resource?” The trade-off is fine-grained, natural permissions versus more facts to model, keep current, and explain when a decision is allowed or denied.

Choose the Facts the Rule Actually Needs

Before choosing an authorization product or language, write the decision as a question. For the rollout plan, the system must answer:

May Nina read document:rollout-plan?

The rule needs several kinds of evidence:

Fact Example Best fit
A stable baseline responsibility project-viewer RBAC
A property of the requester contractor_active=true, device_managed=true ABAC subject attribute
A property of the resource classification=internal, legal_hold=false ABAC resource attribute
A property of the request action=read, risk=normal ABAC action or environment attribute
A connection between entities Nina belongs to partner team; team can view Atlas ReBAC relationship
A structural path document belongs to a folder in Atlas ReBAC relationship

The categories can compose. RBAC may give a product manager a stable baseline role. ReBAC may establish that a user is a viewer of one particular project through a group or parent folder. ABAC may refine that result by checking the document classification and whether a contractor's engagement is active. The models are not competing brand names; they describe different shapes of authorization fact.

An attribute is not just any field a client sends. It needs an owner and a meaning. The identity directory might own contractor_active; the device-management system might own device_managed; the document service might own classification and legal_hold; and a risk service might own request_risk. If the API trusts an unverified is_manager=true claim supplied by the browser, it has turned an input into a permission.

A relationship is likewise an authorization fact, not a convenient string. A relation tuple records one edge such as “user Nina is a member of team Delivery Partners” or “team Delivery Partners is a viewer of project Atlas.” A system may store and query those edges in many ways; ReBAC does not require a graph database. What matters is that the model can traverse an intended path and explain it.

Pause and predict: The same partner team can view one project but not another. Is external-contractor alone enough to allow Nina to read the rollout plan?

Check: No. That attribute says something about Nina, not her connection to Atlas. The decision also needs a relationship such as team membership plus the team's viewer relation to this project, or an equally specific grant.

Worked Path: Viewing a Shared Document

Make the relationship path explicit first. Northstar stores these facts for the Atlas project:

team:delivery-partners#member@user:nina
project:atlas#viewer@team:delivery-partners#member
document:rollout-plan#parent@project:atlas

Read the notation as object#relation@subject. The first tuple says that Nina is a delivery-partners member. The second says that members of that team are viewers of Atlas. The third says that the rollout plan belongs to Atlas. A direct grant would be shorter, such as document:rollout-plan#viewer@user:nina, but the inherited path better reflects why Nina has access and automatically applies to the project's other eligible documents.

The related attributes have their own authoritative sources:

subject attributes from identity and device systems:
  contractor_active = true
  device_managed = true
  sponsor = aria@northstar.example

resource attributes from document service:
  classification = internal
  legal_hold = false

request attributes:
  action = read
  request_risk = normal

The conceptual policy is deliberately small:

allow read when
  user has viewer relationship to document or its project
  AND contractor_active is true
  AND device_managed is true
  AND classification is not restricted
  AND legal_hold is false
  AND request_risk is normal

Now trace Nina's request step by step:

1. API authenticates Nina and identifies document:rollout-plan.
2. Relationship check follows document -> project:atlas -> team:delivery-partners -> Nina.
3. Document service returns classification=internal and legal_hold=false.
4. Identity and device sources return contractor_active=true and device_managed=true.
5. Risk signal is normal for this request.
6. Policy combines the evidence and returns allow.
7. Audit record stores the principal, document, relation path or policy reason, attribute versions, and decision.

The intermediate states explain why a simple role is insufficient. A generic project-viewer role cannot show whether access comes from the Atlas team, whether the document is restricted, or whether Nina's contractor status changed yesterday. The relationship check answers who is connected to what; the attribute checks answer whether this request is still acceptable.

Now change one fact: legal places the rollout plan on hold. The document service changes legal_hold to true. Nina may still be a team member and may still have a managed device, but the policy should deny the next read because the resource's current state has changed. This is not a broken relationship. It is a different input to the same decision.

Pause and predict: Nina's sponsor revokes her engagement, but a cached authorization result says allow for several more hours. Which part of the design is failing?

Check: The relationship or subject attribute may have changed correctly, but the authorization result is too stale for the revocation requirement. The system must define how quickly that source is observed, bound or invalidate cached decisions, and avoid treating an old allow as timeless truth.

So far: ReBAC derives the allowed path to a resource. ABAC tests current conditions around that path. Both are only as reliable as the authority and freshness of the facts they consume.

Freshness, Ownership, and Denial Behavior

Fine-grained authorization turns more domain facts into security inputs. That creates an ownership question for every fact: who may write it, how does a decision service read it, and how stale may it be before access becomes unsafe?

For Atlas, an HR or identity lifecycle system should control whether Nina is an active contractor. The project service should control team-to-project access. The document service should control classification and legal hold. The authorization layer should not silently recreate those facts from a display name, an old token claim, or a client-side cache.

Not every fact needs the same freshness. A public documentation tag might safely lag for a few minutes. A sponsor revocation, a legal hold, or a high-risk device signal may require the next decision to observe the change immediately or to deny until the source is reachable. There is no universal cache time-to-live that makes this correct. The product must state the revocation promise and then choose versions, invalidation, synchronous reads, or short-lived decision tokens that meet it.

Failure behavior is part of the policy. If the device posture service cannot answer, an internal dashboard might show a limited view while a restricted export denies by default. If the relationship store cannot confirm project membership, allowing based on a stale browser claim is rarely a safe fallback. “Unknown” is a meaningful decision state that should have an explicit product response rather than being collapsed into a convenient allow.

This also affects auditing. A useful decision record does not merely say allow Nina rollout-plan. It captures which relationship path matched, which attribute values or versions were used, which source was unavailable, the policy revision, and why the decision was denied when it was. Do not log raw access tokens or unnecessary personal attributes; log enough evidence to reproduce the authorization reasoning safely.

Where These Models Help, and Where They Hurt

Use RBAC when access maps mainly to a durable job function. Use ABAC when the rule depends on properties that vary by subject, object, action, or environment. Use ReBAC when permission depends on membership, ownership, delegation, hierarchy, sharing, or containment. A real system commonly combines all three.

The benefit is expressive precision. You can grant Nina access because her team is connected to this project, while still denying a restricted document or an unmanaged device. The cost is that the policy now depends on multiple services, schemas, and update paths. A renamed group, a missing attribute, an accidental parent relation, or a stale cache can change access in ways no individual role review reveals.

Avoid two opposite mistakes. The first is role explosion: inventing a role for every project, customer, document state, and exception. The second is policy fog: placing every business rule in a large expression nobody can explain, test, or audit. Prefer a small, named relationship model, a clear list of attribute authorities, and policies that can produce a reason for their decision.

ReBAC can also expose non-obvious paths. If a document inherits view permission from a folder, and the folder inherits from a workspace, a reviewer needs a way to see that chain. If a group membership is removed, the team needs to know which documents lose access and when the change becomes visible. Those questions lead naturally to the policy engines, tuple stores, testing, and caching lessons later in this track; they should not be deferred until an incident.

Design Check

Start with a nearby case. A contractor needs to comment on issues in the atlas project while their sponsor is active. They may not view restricted design documents or export issue data from an unmanaged device.

relationships:
  contractor is a member of delivery-partners
  delivery-partners is a commenter of project atlas
  issue belongs to project atlas

attributes:
  contractor_active = true
  issue.classification != restricted
  device_managed = true
  action = comment or export

policy result:
  comment may be allowed through the relationship path and attributes
  export is denied on an unmanaged device

This design keeps the project connection in relationships and the changing conditions in attributes. It does not create a permanent atlas-contractor-exporter role.

Now choose one permission from a product you know and write:

request: principal, action, resource
stable role, if any:
relationship facts and their owner:
subject, resource, and environment attributes and their owner:
freshness or revocation promise:
deny behavior when a required fact is unknown:
decision evidence to retain:

Use this rubric: every fact must have a named authority; the relationship path must be explainable; a changing condition must not be hidden in a permanent role; and the design must say what happens when a security-relevant fact is stale or unavailable.

Connections

Key Takeaways

  • RBAC describes stable responsibilities; ABAC evaluates current subject, resource, action, and environment facts; ReBAC evaluates paths of ownership, membership, sharing, and hierarchy.
  • An attribute or relationship can grant access only when it has an authoritative source, a defined meaning, and an appropriate freshness guarantee.
  • Combine models deliberately: use relationships to establish resource connection and attributes to constrain that connection under current conditions.
  • Fine-grained policies buy precision but require explainable paths, decision evidence, and explicit behavior for stale or unavailable security facts.
