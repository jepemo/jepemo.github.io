008 30 min intermediate CAPSTONE

Capstone: Comprehensive Integration and Architecture Review

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to...

  • Produce a compact architecture review that connects product promises, authority, coordination scope, operating pressure, and failure behavior.

  • Defend a proposed boundary with normal and degraded traces, credible alternatives, and rollout evidence.

  • Recommend whether a redesign should proceed, narrow, or stop based on stated readiness criteria.

Idea in one sentence: A design is ready to proceed when every important mechanism has a named pressure, owner, cost, failure signal, and bounded rollout path.

Core Insight

The hard part of an architecture review is not recognizing familiar components. It is deciding which promises need authority, which paths may wait, and which new cost is justified by evidence. The same system can legitimately use a local room coordinator, a durable record, a background worker, and an edge cache because those roles answer different questions.

For the learning platform, a low-latency cursor, a committed board edit, an accepted progress update, and a search result do not deserve the same coordination cost. The review earns its conclusion by making those differences visible. A component is only a good answer when the reviewer can say what it protects, what it cannot protect, and how an operator would recognize that its boundary is failing.

The Scenario

You are reviewing a redesign for a learning platform with collaborative whiteboards. Learners join workshops from browsers, mobiles, and occasionally offline editors. They draw together, complete lessons, receive notifications, search for boards, and export snapshots.

The current system handled small workshops well. It now struggles during large public sessions. One board can attract thousands of viewers. Export workers compete with interactive traffic. Search may lag after a launch. Learners sometimes see progress later on a second device. Permission changes must remain strict even while collaboration feels fast.

The team has proposed a diagram with regional gateways, a board edit log, a progress event path, worker pools, an edge cache, tenant limits, and a small operations-automation policy. The diagram is plausible. This capstone asks a harder question: does each part earn its place, and can the team introduce it without hiding its cost?

The initial model says a comprehensive review should mention every component. That model breaks quickly. A diagram can contain many correct boxes while leaving the important decisions unanswered. The stronger model is selective: begin with promises and pressure, then require a reason for every boundary that changes authority, waiting, recovery, or operational ownership.

Constraints

Treat the following as the decision frame. They are deliberately concrete enough to challenge.

Authoritative promises:
  accepted progress must not disappear after acknowledgment;
  committed board edits need one recoverable order per board;
  a revoked permission must prevent protected access under the stated freshness rule.

Experience promises:
  active collaborators should see accepted strokes within the agreed latency target;
  a board save should not wait for notifications, analytics, or exports;
  static application assets should load efficiently for distant users.

Derived promises:
  search, analytics, notifications, and exports may lag or retry under an
  explicit product policy.

Operating constraints:
  a large workshop must not exhaust capacity needed by unrelated customers;
  regional latency and worker backlog are expected failure modes;
  the team needs a reversible rollout, not one all-at-once migration.

The constraints distinguish strict facts from useful delay. A search result that appears late is a freshness problem. An accepted progress record that disappears is a durability failure. A stale permission can become an access-control failure. Naming these differences prevents the system from paying global coordination cost for every path, or from accidentally treating an important promise as best effort.

Design Goal

The goal is not “make the platform distributed.” It is to keep three promises together:

1. protect durable user work and access control;
2. keep interactive collaboration responsive under a large-room pressure;
3. move delay-tolerant work out of the acceptance path while making that delay visible.

This goal creates tension. A regional session gateway may improve interactive latency, but it cannot quietly become the authority for permissions or committed board history. An asynchronous worker can isolate notification work, but it adds lag, duplicates, and backlog operations. An edge cache can improve static delivery, but it must not decide a newly changed access rule.

The architecture review therefore asks a recurring question: what is the smallest scope with enough information and authority to keep this particular promise? That is the coordination lens from lesson 003, now applied to one full system.

Proposed Model

The proposed model assigns roles before it assigns products:

Role Proposed boundary Why it exists
Authoritative board history board-scoped durable edit log preserves committed order and replay for one board
Authoritative progress durable accepted-progress record keeps an acknowledged update recoverable
Authoritative access policy ownership and permission service makes protected access decisions explicit
Interactive coordination regional board session gateway reduces latency for active collaboration
Acceleration edge cache for versioned assets and permitted snapshots reduces safe read delivery cost
Derived work workers for notifications, search, analytics, exports isolates work that may lag from acceptance
Protection from bursts per-board and per-tenant limits prevents one workload from consuming shared capacity
Bounded response pre-approved playbooks or automation for derived work protects the user path during known incidents

This table is a teaching design, not a claim that each role needs a separate deployable service. A modular application could host several roles. What matters is that the review can say which role owns a fact, which role makes a path faster, and which role follows after acceptance.

The central decision is to keep authoritative state separate from derived state. A queue or worker may deliver a consequence of an accepted event. It does not decide whether the event counts. A cache may speed up a safe read. It does not silently become the current permission authority.

Walkthrough

Trace one realistic workshop path. The sequence makes intermediate states inspectable.

Starting state:
  Mateo has permission to board B;
  board B has committed edit sequence 100;
  Mateo has not completed lesson 008;
  search is allowed to be a few seconds behind.

Step 1: Connect
  Mateo connects through a nearby regional session gateway. The gateway obtains
  current access through the authoritative policy path before protected use.

Step 2: Collaborate
  Mateo draws a shape. The gateway coordinates the live room interaction and
  the board authority commits the accepted edit as sequence 101.

Step 3: Recover
  The committed edit remains available for replay if Mateo reconnects. A local
  presence update may have been visible earlier, but it is not the durable history.

Step 4: Complete progress
  Mateo completes the capstone. The Progress API validates the request and
  writes the accepted progress record durably before returning success.

Step 5: Project and derive
  A progress view on another device updates after acceptance. Notification,
  search, analytics, and export workers consume derived work later.

Step 6: Accelerate safe reads
  The browser loads versioned static code from an edge cache. A permitted,
  read-only snapshot may use a cache policy; a permission change does not.

Two intermediate states deserve explicit policy. After live room coordination but before the durable board commit, a cursor or provisional shape may be visible. That is acceptable only if the product makes the provisional nature clear and recovery has a rule. After durable progress acceptance but before a projection updates another device, the second device may be briefly stale. That is acceptable only if the stated convergence window and mismatch handling make it acceptable.

So far, the architecture has not promised instant global agreement. It has promised the right kind of agreement at the right boundary, and visible delay elsewhere.

Check: A notification worker fails immediately after Mateo's progress record is accepted. Should the completion response be reversed?

Think first, then reveal.

Answer: No. The accepted progress record is authoritative and durable. Notification delivery is derived work. The worker failure must create backlog, retry, and product-policy behavior, but it must not rewrite whether the learner completed the lesson.

Failure Review

Now apply pressure. One enterprise workshop produces ten times normal board activity. Export workers consume shared capacity and notification backlog age grows. The review must protect the important promises before improving every metric.

What must remain true:
  committed board edits and accepted progress remain durable;
  permission enforcement remains within its stated freshness rule.

What may lag or shed:
  exports, analytics, notifications, and search indexing under policy.

What must stay responsive:
  active collaboration and acceptance paths within their target budgets.

First controls to consider:
  per-board backpressure; tenant-level capacity visibility; reduced export
  concurrency; worker pause for noncritical derived work; backlog alerts.

The tempting reaction is to scale every component or to retry more aggressively. That can waste capacity and amplify overload. Lesson 004's constraint hypothesis comes first: trace waiting by room size, region, and path. If fanout queues dominate active-editor latency, use a bounded viewer-relay experiment. If durable append dominates, relays will not fix the incident. If a shared worker pool is saturated, protect the interactive and authoritative paths before adding more retries.

Bounded automation may pause background exports when the policy and evidence permit it. It may not alter ownership, discard committed state, bypass permissions, or silence alerts. The automation boundary is an authority decision, not a claim that automation is always better.

Trade-offs and Alternatives

The proposed model is not the only plausible design. Review alternatives by their losses as well as their gains.

Alternative Why it is attractive Why it loses under these constraints
Keep one synchronous request path fewer moving parts and immediate visibility derived worker failure can delay or fail accepted user work
Globally coordinate every board interaction one broad ordering story remote coordination and tail latency charge every low-latency interaction
Use the proposed scoped model authority stays durable; derived work is isolated; interactive scope stays small more roles, backlog operations, replay rules, and rollout complexity
Move all authority to the edge attractive locality permission and durable-history ownership become difficult to reason about and recover

The scoped model earns its cost when the measured pressures are real: large-room fanout, downstream coupling, and geographic interaction delay. It becomes premature if the platform has only small rooms, no derived backlog, and no evidence that central routing harms the user path. A smaller mechanism is not less mature. It is often the more responsible choice.

The model also has limits. A board-scoped log does not automatically solve offline conflict resolution. A cache policy does not make access control safe by itself. An asynchronous worker does not guarantee downstream success. A relay does not remove the need to replay or reconcile. The review should state these boundaries so later teams do not read “introduced a component” as “solved a category.”

Decisions Still Open

The review should not pretend that every uncertainty is resolved. It should identify the questions that need evidence before a broader rollout. For this design, three decisions remain deliberately open.

First, decide the exact freshness rule for permission changes. “Check permissions authoritatively” is not a complete rule until the team says how a revocation reaches a regional gateway, what the maximum acceptable stale interval is, and what happens when the policy path is unavailable. The safe default may be to deny protected access when freshness cannot be established, but that choice has an availability cost that product and security owners must accept together.

Second, decide the recovery rule for offline board edits. A board-scoped committed order protects an accepted online history. It does not by itself define how an editor's offline change merges after the board has advanced. That is not a reason to abandon the current boundary. It is a reason to keep offline merge semantics out of this rollout unless the design includes a separate conflict model, user-visible resolution rule, and replay test.

Third, decide the policy for stale derived outputs. A notification that arrives twenty minutes late may be useful, useless, or harmful depending on its purpose. An exported snapshot may be regenerated. A search result may wait within a stated freshness budget. Each derived path needs a policy that says when to retry, compact, drop, or ask for operator action. A queue gives delivery attempts; it does not choose the product meaning of lateness.

These open decisions are a sign of a disciplined review, not a weak one. They mark the boundary between what the current evidence supports and what needs another proposal, experiment, or owner decision.

Design Defense

Imagine a reviewer asks, “Why not make every write globally coordinated so we never have to reason about intermediate states?” The answer is that global coordination would spend latency and availability on paths whose promise may not require it. A live cursor and a passive viewer update can tolerate different delay than a committed edit or permission revocation. The review is not choosing inconsistency by default. It is choosing the lightest sufficient coordination for each promise and making the remaining uncertainty observable.

Another reviewer asks, “Why not keep the entire path synchronous so nothing lags?” That approach buys a simple immediate story while downstream work stays quick. Under load, it makes a notification provider, analytics sink, or export worker part of the learner's save path. The proposed boundary accepts a different cost: workers can lag, retry, and require operation. That cost is justified only if the team actually measures downstream coupling and commits to backlog, replay, and duplicate-effect controls.

Finally, a reviewer asks whether automation should pause workers during an incident. The answer depends on authority. Pre-approved throttling of noncritical exports can protect an interactive promise when signals show a known pressure. Changing permission policy or discarding accepted edits crosses a much stronger boundary and needs human authority or a separately verified control. This distinction keeps the design from quietly expanding a useful mitigation into a new source of systemic risk.

The design can therefore proceed only in the areas its evidence earns: bounded regional interaction, durable acceptance, derived-work isolation, and explicit capacity controls. Other moves stay hypotheses until their own promises, failure modes, and rollout evidence are reviewed.

Evidence and Readiness

Before rollout, turn every promise and risk into an observable signal:

User outcomes:
  p99 active-editor delivery by room size and region;
  accepted progress-save latency; cross-device projection freshness.

Authority and recovery:
  edit sequence gaps; replay success; progress idempotency failures;
  permission freshness or denied-access anomalies.

Derived-work safety:
  backlog age; retry and dead-letter rate; duplicate notification or export rate.

Capacity and rollout:
  per-tenant saturation; relay errors; old-versus-new projection mismatches;
  cohort error rate; rollback trigger status.

Metrics are not decoration. Each one tests a promise or a new risk. An average across every board can hide the hot-room tail. A healthy queue dashboard can hide a slow user path. A fast gateway can hide an unsafe permission cache. Split signals by the conditions that created the decision.

Use a reversible rollout:

1. instrument the old paths and record a baseline;
2. shadow the new event or projection path and compare outcomes;
3. enable one bounded mechanism for a small eligible cohort;
4. expand only while user outcomes and safety guardrails remain healthy;
5. stop new routing and reconcile already accepted facts if a rollback trigger fires;
6. remove the old path only after representative traffic and incident drills.

Rollback is not erasure. Facts already accepted in a durable path may require draining or reconciliation. The plan must say which traffic can return to the old path, which state needs compatibility, and who owns the decision during an incident.

Final Challenge: Write the Review Memo

Write a one-page architecture review for the proposed redesign. Do not list every technology. Defend whether the redesign should proceed, narrow, or stop.

Your memo should include:

Decision and pressure:
  the user or operating condition that makes a change necessary.

Promises and authority:
  the authoritative facts, derived work, acceptable lag, and owner for each fact.

Chosen boundary and alternatives:
  the lightest mechanism that matches the pressure, plus why credible options lost.

Normal and degraded traces:
  one accepted path, one failure path, and the intermediate states that matter.

Evidence and rollout:
  baseline, metrics, guardrails, cohort, rollback rule, and accepted cost.

Use this rubric:

A strong memo A memo not yet ready
Names a concrete pressure and product promise Starts with a component or fashionable technology
Separates authoritative, accelerated, and derived roles Lets a cache, queue, or projection become accidental authority
Shows a normal trace and a degraded trace Shows only the happy-path diagram
States alternatives and their accepted losses Claims the chosen design is simply best
Includes metrics, ownership, and a reversible rollout Says “we can scale” or “we can roll back” without evidence

One reasonable recommendation is: proceed with the scoped model in stages, but begin with evidence for the actual large-room and downstream-coupling pressures. Keep board and progress authority durable, treat searches and notifications as derived under visible lag policies, and limit automation to pre-approved controls over noncritical work. Stop or narrow the rollout if replay gaps, permission anomalies, or user-facing tail latency cross their guardrails.

Key Takeaways

  • A comprehensive review is selective: every boundary must earn its cost by protecting a promise or relieving measured pressure.
  • Authority, acceleration, and derivation are different roles; confusing them turns useful performance layers into correctness risks.
  • Normal and degraded traces expose intermediate states, recovery rules, and product policies that a diagram alone cannot show.
  • Readiness requires evidence, operational ownership, and a rollout that can stop new behavior without denying already accepted facts.
