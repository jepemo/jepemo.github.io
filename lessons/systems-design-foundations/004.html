Optimization, Constraints, and Real-World Trade-offs

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to...

  • Turn a report that a system is “slow” into one user-visible path and a measurable symptom.

  • Form and test a constraint hypothesis from a trace instead of optimizing the most visible component.

  • Propose a bounded change with a success metric, a new risk, and a rollback or stop condition.

Idea in one sentence: An optimization is useful when evidence shows that it relieves the waiting that breaks a specific promise, and when the team can own the cost it moves elsewhere.

Core Insight

At 09:12, teachers open a shared whiteboard for a large class. Priya draws a stroke. Her own browser shows it immediately, but many students see it arrive almost a second later. The team receives the familiar report: “the board is slow.”

That report is a symptom, not a diagnosis. It might mean that the browser is slow, that one room owner is busy, that a network hop is long, or that thousands of viewers are waiting behind the same fanout queue. The words are the same. The useful response is not.

The tempting model is simple: find a piece of code that looks hot and make it faster. That works when local computation really dominates a small path. It fails when the user is mostly waiting for coordination, queues, storage, or a slow downstream client. In a distributed system, a three-millisecond handler can sit inside a 900-millisecond experience.

The stronger model is a constraint hypothesis. First name the promise that failed. Then trace the path that keeps that promise. Measure the meaningful waits. Only then change the part that is limiting the result. The technical word is bottleneck, but active constraint is often more helpful: it reminds us that CPU is only one possible limit.

The Production Symptom

The product promise for this incident is narrow:

When a teacher draws on a large board,
active collaborators should see the accepted stroke quickly enough to keep up.

This is not the same promise as “search is fresh” or “a new board is created quickly.” Lesson 002 separated those promises; lesson 003 separated the scopes that serve them. Here, the operational job is to discover which part of this one live-collaboration path is under pressure.

The team records an illustrative incident window. These numbers are a teaching example, not production measurements:

Signal Small boards Boards with more than 1,000 viewers
p99 stroke delivery to active editors 95 ms 920 ms
handler CPU time 3 ms 4 ms
room fanout queue wait 12 ms 410 ms
slow-viewer buffer wait 8 ms 320 ms
accepted-event append 35 ms 42 ms

The user sees delay. The system sees where that delay accumulates. The handler is not free, but shaving one millisecond from it cannot explain or repair hundreds of milliseconds of queueing.

What The System Knows

Make the path visible before choosing an intervention:

Priya's browser
  -> gateway
  -> board 84 session owner
  -> validate current board access
  -> assign a room sequence
  -> fan out to active collaborators and viewers
  -> record accepted stroke for reconnect
  -> send derived work to search and analytics

The trace has several promises. The room sequence and accepted record protect the collaboration and recovery promises. Search and analytics are derived work; they should not decide whether the stroke is visible. A notification worker that is late may be a problem, but it is not evidence for this latency incident.

For this lesson, assume Priya has already been authorized and the room can accept her stroke. Those are explicit teaching assumptions. A real investigation would also test access checks, retries, regional routing, and reconnect behavior if their signals pointed there.

Check: The accepted-event append rises from 35 ms to 42 ms. Is it the active constraint?

Think first, then reveal.

Answer: Not from this trace. It contributes to the path, so it still needs a budget. But the much larger waits are fanout scheduling and slow-viewer buffering. The active constraint is the stage whose improvement would materially change the failed user metric.

Investigation Path

The first hypothesis should be precise enough to lose.

Weak hypothesis:
  The collaboration service is overloaded.

Testable hypothesis:
  On boards above 1,000 viewers, p99 delivery to active editors is dominated by
  fanout scheduling and queues for slow viewers, rather than handler CPU or
  durable append latency.

The second statement gives the team a way to be wrong. It names a condition, a user-visible metric, the suspected waits, and competing explanations. Add the observations that could reject it:

If we observe… Then the hypothesis becomes weaker because…
Fanout time stays low while durable append dominates p99 The wait is not mainly in fanout.
Only one region is affected before the session owner Routing or network locality may be the pressure.
Queue depth rises on every room, including small ones A shared resource or a broader overload may be involved.
Active-editor and viewer delivery are equally delayed with empty buffers The split between those paths may not be the useful boundary.

This is why “add more servers” is not yet a plan. More API servers may help a saturated gateway. They do not automatically increase the capacity of the session owner or prevent a slow viewer from holding fanout work hostage.

The Failure Mechanism

The naive fanout design sends every accepted stroke through one room owner and treats every recipient alike. It is easy to explain and may work well for a small room.

It breaks when passive viewers greatly outnumber active editors. A viewer on a weak connection can accumulate buffered work. If the room owner gives that viewer the same immediate delivery path as the teacher, scheduling and buffers can delay the interaction that must feel live.

The problem is not that viewers matter less. It is that the system has two different freshness needs:

active editor:
  needs fast, ordered feedback for the current interaction

passive viewer:
  may accept coalesced updates or a slightly later view under an explicit policy

This is a design choice under named constraints, not a universal split. A trading screen, a medical monitor, or a collaborative editor with strict shared-state rules may need a different delivery promise. The product contract decides what delay or coalescing is acceptable.

Mitigation and Prevention

For the observed hot-room pressure, the team can test a bounded change:

Change:
  keep the room owner as the authority that sequences accepted strokes;
  send active editors through its direct low-latency path;
  send large-room viewers through relays that can coalesce non-critical updates.

Expected benefit:
  reduce p99 active-editor delivery below 180 ms on boards above 1,000 viewers.

Guardrails:
  accepted strokes remain replayable;
  a relay cannot create or reorder authoritative room state;
  a viewer can resynchronize after a relay gap.

This move uses lesson 003's distinction. The session owner remains authority. Relays are an acceleration layer for a particular delivery path. Search and analytics stay derived work. Naming those roles prevents a fast cache or relay from quietly becoming the source of truth.

Roll out the change only to boards that meet the incident condition. Compare a control group with the new path. Stop or roll back if p99 does not improve enough, replay gaps rise, or relay errors exceed the agreed budget. A rollout is an experiment, not a declaration that the theory was right.

The New Cost We Must Own

The relay changes where work waits; it does not erase work. It introduces new questions:

Improvement New cost or failure boundary Signal to watch
Faster active-editor path Relays can fall behind or fail relay lag and reconnect resync rate
Less work per slow viewer Viewers may see coalesced updates viewer freshness percentile
More fanout capacity Ordering and replay rules are harder to inspect sequence-gap and replay-failure rate
Targeted large-room treatment Two delivery modes add operational complexity rollout error rate by room size

Caching illustrates the same rule. A metadata read cache can reduce repeated lookup latency, but it may serve stale authorization. More retries can hide a transient failure, but they can amplify overload. Sharding can raise throughput, but it adds routing and skew. Each intervention should name the promise it helps and the promise or cost it can endanger.

An optimization is complete when the chosen promise meets its target and its guardrails remain healthy. It is not complete merely because a dashboard number improved. If the system moves the pain from editor latency to unrecoverable gaps, it did not improve the product.

Signals to Watch

Use a small operational panel tied to the hypothesis:

Primary outcome:
  p99 delivery latency for active editors on large boards

Constraint signals:
  fanout queue wait; per-recipient buffer depth; relay utilization

Safety signals:
  accepted-stroke replay gaps; relay retry/error rate; reconnect resync time

Scope check:
  the same signals split by room size and region

Splitting the signals matters. An average across every board can hide the large-room tail that triggered the change. Similarly, a dashboard that reports relay health without the original user metric can make a new component look healthy while users still wait.

Readiness Check

Review this proposal before implementation:

Users say the board is slow.
We will add API servers and rewrite the serializer.
Success means average CPU falls by 30%.

Check: What makes this proposal unready?

Answer: It does not name the failing user path or condition. It assumes CPU is the constraint without trace evidence. Its success metric is an average component metric, not the p99 delivery promise. It also has no stated cost, safety signal, or rollback condition.

Rewrite it with this compact form:

Symptom and condition:
  [who sees what delay, and when]

Evidence and hypothesis:
  [which trace stages wait, and what would disprove this]

Bounded change:
  [one intervention and its authority boundary]

Success and safety:
  [one user outcome, one guardrail, and a stop or rollback condition]

Practice: Diagnose a Nearby Path

Search results are twenty minutes behind after a course launch, while live drawing remains healthy. Write a constraint hypothesis and one targeted change.

A good answer should mention:

  • the failing promise is derived search freshness, not live collaboration;
  • evidence such as queue age, worker throughput, failed jobs, batch delay, and search commit latency;
  • a bounded intervention such as partitioning or adding worker capacity only after the evidence identifies the limiting stage;
  • a new cost such as hot partitions, duplicate work, reduced batching efficiency, or higher cost per event;
  • a success metric and a guardrail, for example freshness percentile plus retry or duplicate-index rate.

Key Takeaways

  • A report that a system is “slow” becomes actionable only when it names a user-visible path, condition, and metric.
  • The active constraint is where the path is meaningfully waiting now; it may be coordination or queueing rather than local CPU.
  • A constraint hypothesis should be specific enough to be disproved by traces or signals.
  • Every optimization moves work and risk somewhere else, so success needs both an outcome metric and a safety guardrail.
