Capstone: Build a Comparative Great Books Commonplace

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to...

  • Build a comparative commonplace from at least three works and two track clusters.

  • Support a cross-work claim with passage cards, formal mechanisms, context notes, counterevidence, and stated limits.

  • Produce a rereading plan that explains how a later passage could revise your judgment.

Idea in one sentence: A comparative commonplace is not a trophy shelf of quotations; it is a small system for showing how your reading question, evidence, and judgment change one another.

Core Insight

Consider a familiar failure. You finish several difficult books and collect impressive sentences in one document. Months later, the document proves that you encountered Tolstoy, Dostoevsky, Kafka, Camus, Nabokov, Orwell, and Borges. It does not show what you noticed, where the words came from, why they matter together, or whether any later reading changed your mind.

A commonplace solves a narrower problem. It gives a reading question somewhere to live long enough to meet evidence that resists it. The unit is not an author, a prestige label, or a perfect quotation. It is a passage card: a bounded scene or passage connected to an observation, a formal mechanism, a provisional claim, and a reason the claim might fail.

This capstone asks you to build a comparative commonplace dossier. You will use at least three works from at least two clusters of the track. You will bring the earlier artifacts together without pretending they are the same tool:

  • the entry card from lesson 001;
  • the agency-at-scale map from lesson 002;
  • the voice-and-idea matrix from lesson 003;
  • the comparative dossier from lesson 004;
  • the verdict-and-response matrix from lesson 005;
  • the style-and-ethics annotation from lesson 006; and
  • the form-and-stakes map from lesson 007.

The goal is not to use every label on every passage. The goal is to choose the few lenses that make your question more answerable.

Choose a Living Question, Not a Theme Drawer

Start with a question that can be changed by evidence. “What do these books say about life?” is too large. “How does literature explore guilt?” is usually a theme drawer: it collects material but gives no test for comparison.

Use a question with pressure and a boundary. For example:

  • When does an explanation help a character act, and when does it make action harder?
  • How does a voice turn moral certainty into a problem rather than a solution?
  • When does a system of representation clarify responsibility, and when does it hide it?
  • How does narrative form make a reader share, resist, or revise a judgment?

Plain meaning:

A living question is specific enough to guide selection and open enough to be revised. It tells you what kind of evidence you need, but not what conclusion you must reach.

In this capstone:

If your question concerns explanation and action, a Tolstoy scene can show intention inside many causes; a Kafka scene can show an institution that withholds a usable verdict; a Borges scene can show a system of knowledge that delays the decision it promised to support. The works are not comparable because they are all “about meaning.” They are comparable because each pressures one question through a different form.

Technical name:

Call the final artifact a comparative commonplace dossier. It contains passage cards, a mechanism matrix, a revised claim, counterreadings, and a rereading plan. Its order matters: evidence before conclusion, difference before synthesis, and revision before certainty.

The Small Dossier Before the Large Claim

Use three passage cards. Each card should fit on one screen or page. Do not reproduce a large passage; use a short quotation where your edition permits, or a precise paraphrase with a location.

Card field What to record
Work and location Title, author, edition or translation, chapter/page/section, and a bounded scene.
Observation What is visibly said, done, repeated, withheld, or formally arranged?
Mechanism Which lens helps: scale, voice, verdict, style, or form? State the mechanism in plain language.
First claim What does this scene seem to show about your living question?
Counterdetail What detail makes that first claim incomplete, unstable, or too broad?
Context note One piece of historical, translation, or genre context only if it changes the reading.
Uncertainty What later scene, edition difference, or missing evidence would you need to inspect?

The edition field is not bureaucracy. A translation can alter a repeated word, a tone, or a relation of formality. Record enough information that you can return to the same textual event. Context should be small and useful. “This author lived in the twentieth century” does not change a reading. “This edition uses a different word for the official charge” might.

A Worked Comparative Commonplace

Here is a miniature dossier built from invented practice analogues. They are not evidence about the authors; they show how the artifact moves.

Living question: When does a system that promises explanation make action harder?

Card Observation and mechanism First claim Counterdetail and uncertainty
A: Tolstoy-style bridge scene A colonel orders a bridge cleared, but mud, a broken wagon, villagers, and a misread time redirect the outcome. Use the agency-at-scale map. An intention cannot explain an event by itself. The colonel still makes choices that affect others. A later scene might show whether the dispersed causes reduce, clarify, or redirect responsibility.
B: Kafka-style appeal scene An official says an appeal has been reviewed but gives no charge, evidence, reviewer, or next step. Use the verdict-and-response matrix. A system of explanation can become oppressive when it acts without becoming legible. The official may be lying, mistaken, or part of a larger procedure. Another scene could reveal whether any appeal is possible.
C: Borges-style archive scene Every guide points to a more precise guide, while a visitor misses her train. Use the form-and-stakes map. The desire for complete representation can displace the action it was meant to support. The archive may eventually yield a usable map, or the visitor may choose another kind of knowledge. The rule's literal limit remains unknown.

Now compare mechanisms, not plot objects.

Work cluster What promises explanation? What blocks or complicates it? What changes for action?
Tolstoy An order and an account of causation. Many agents, conditions, and sequences intervene. Judgment must be distributed without erasing responsibility.
Kafka A procedure and implied verdict. Authority withholds charge, evidence, ownership, and remedy. Response continues, but its target is unstable.
Borges A perfectly precise guide. Each clarification produces another representation. The search for total knowledge competes with timely choice.

The first synthesis might be: These works show that explanation is always a trap. That is a theme drawer. It ignores their differences and cannot tell us what evidence would challenge it.

A revised claim is stronger:

Each scene makes explanation difficult in a different way: Tolstoy distributes causation across a social sequence, Kafka stages authority that will not disclose its judgment, and Borges turns precision into a recursive delay. None simply rejects explanation; each asks what form of explanation permits or prevents responsible action.

This claim can be revised. A passage in which an explanation genuinely enables repair would qualify it. A change of translation might change whether an official's language sounds evasive or merely formal. A fourth work might show style, rather than institution or form, making explanation feel trustworthy. The dossier is working because it has places for these changes to land.

After writing three cards, make one matrix. Use only the columns that earn their place.

The matrix is the worked path of the capstone:

  1. Input: a living question and three bounded passages.
  2. Transition: assign a lens and trace the formal pressure in each passage.
  3. Intermediate state: write three local claims that do not yet agree.
  4. Decision: write one comparison that names a similarity and at least one formal difference.
  5. Naive failure contrast: replace “all these books say X” with a claim that can explain why the mechanisms are not interchangeable.

If every row has the same answer, your matrix is probably too vague. Return to the passage cards. If every row uses a different lens but never touches the question, narrow the question or replace a passage. Coherence is not forced agreement. It is a visible reason the works belong in the same inquiry.

Use Context and Translation as Revision Tools

Context is useful when it changes a reading decision. It is not a decorative biography paragraph.

Suppose your claim depends on a word that appears in a translation of a Dostoevsky or Kafka passage. Record the edition and ask whether another translation gives the same relation of insult, obligation, guilt, or authority. You do not need to become a translator to do this responsibly. You need to mark the claim as edition-bound until you have compared it.

Suppose a political phrase in Orwell changes your understanding of what a narrator or institution calls “necessary.” Record the phrase's immediate rhetorical work before importing a large historical explanation. Suppose a Borges invented document imitates scholarship. Describe what the imitation authorizes or destabilizes inside the fiction before treating it as a report about Borges's biography.

The trade-off is that context and translation notes make the dossier slower. They also prevent false certainty. The boundary appears when context replaces the passage: if your note could stand without the scene, it is probably not serving this commonplace.

Common Failure Modes

Failure: The prestige shelf

Why it happens:

Well-known titles can feel like evidence by themselves.

Repair:

Every card needs a location, observation, mechanism, and counterdetail. A famous author without a bounded passage is not a comparison point yet.

Failure: Matching themes, flattening forms

Why it happens:

Words such as freedom, guilt, truth, and absurdity travel easily.

Repair:

State what each form does before naming a shared theme. A distributed event, contested voice, opaque verdict, seductive narrator, and recursive archive may all pressure judgment, but not by the same route.

Failure: The perfect final claim

Why it happens:

A conclusion can feel more satisfying when it sounds complete.

Repair:

Add a counterreading and a rereading trigger. Say what passage, edition, or context note could alter the claim. This is not weakness. It is the evidence of an interpretation that can learn.

Failure: The quote museum

Why it happens:

Beautiful lines are easy to save and hard to reconnect to a scene.

Repair:

Write the observation and mechanism before copying a quotation. End every card with a question that sends you back to the work.

Check Your Understanding

Check: A dossier compares War and Peace, The Trial, and a Borges story only by saying that all three “show that humans are powerless.” What is the best repair?

Think first, then reveal.

Answer: Return to three bounded scenes and identify their distinct formal pressures: distributed causation, inaccessible procedure, and a rule of representation. Then revise the claim to say how each makes agency or judgment difficult, and add a counterdetail that keeps each local reading provisional.

Check: Which context note is most likely to improve a passage card?

  • A. “The author is widely considered important.”
  • B. “This passage is in a novel.”
  • C. “My edition translates the official's repeated term as ‘charge’; another edition I consulted uses ‘accusation,’ so my claim about legal procedure remains tentative.”

Think first, then reveal.

Answer: C. It identifies a specific difference that could change the interpretation. A and B may be true, but they do not yet alter the reading decision.

Capstone Practice: Assemble the Commonplace

Build a comparative commonplace dossier with these required parts:

  1. Living question: one question of 20–35 words.
  2. Three passage cards: from at least three works and two track clusters. Include location, observation, lens, first claim, counterdetail, context note when needed, and uncertainty.
  3. Mechanism matrix: one row per work, including a formal difference.
  4. Comparative claim: 120–180 words. State a similarity, at least one difference, and the consequence for your question.
  5. Counterreading: 60–100 words explaining how one card or another plausible interpretation resists your main claim.
  6. Rereading plan: name two passages, editions, or contextual questions you will inspect next and say exactly what each could change.

Use this rubric. Score each category from 0 to 2.

Category 0 1 2
Evidence Author names or unlocated quotations. Located passages with thin observation. Bounded passages with precise observation and return paths.
Formal precision Theme matching only. One mechanism is visible. Each work has a distinct, passage-based formal pressure.
Comparison One verdict for all works. Similarity or difference appears. Similarity and formal difference change the final claim.
Context and translation Biography or prestige replaces reading. Context is present but loose. Context or edition note changes a specific reading decision.
Revision No counterevidence. A generic caveat. A real counterreading and a stated rereading trigger.
Human stakes Formal devices are treated as puzzles. Stakes are named generally. The dossier shows what attention, judgment, relation, or action the form changes.

Aim for 9 or more out of 12 before calling the dossier complete. A score below that is not a verdict on your intelligence or taste. It tells you where to reread: locate the passages, sharpen a mechanism, or replace a claim that has become too broad.

Key Takeaways

  • A comparative commonplace organizes reading around a living question, not a canon list or theme drawer.
  • Passage cards turn observations into revisable claims by recording mechanism, counterdetail, context, and uncertainty.
  • A mechanism matrix makes formal differences visible before a cross-work conclusion is written.
  • The strongest capstone claim can say what would revise it and where the next rereading should begin.
