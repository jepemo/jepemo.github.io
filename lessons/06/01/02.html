Day 102: Service Decomposition and Boundary Design

The hardest part of microservices is not creating services; it is deciding where a boundary belongs and what each boundary is allowed to own.

Today's "Aha!" Moment

The insight: Good service decomposition is driven by business boundaries and ownership, not by superficial technical slicing such as "one service per table" or "one service per CRUD area."

Why this matters: Once a boundary becomes a service boundary, it becomes a deployment boundary, an ownership boundary, and often a data boundary. A bad cut is expensive to undo.

Concrete anchor: The learning platform could be decomposed into identity, course catalog, enrollment, billing, and notifications. The question is not only whether these names sound reasonable, but whether each boundary owns a coherent business capability and can evolve without constant cross-service chatter.

The universal pattern: Complex domain + need for ownership and independent evolution -> careful boundary design around business capability.

How to recognize when this applies:

  • Several modules change for different business reasons.
  • Teams argue frequently about which part of the system owns a concept.
  • Data ownership is fuzzy.
  • Workflows cross modules in confusing ways.
  • Splitting by technology layer or table would create heavy coordination.

Common misconceptions:

  • [INCORRECT] "One service per database table is clean decomposition."
  • [INCORRECT] "Smaller services are always better services."
  • [CORRECT] The truth: Service boundaries should cluster behavior, data, and ownership around a coherent capability. Too-small boundaries often create coordination overhead without real autonomy.

Real-world examples:

  1. Identity service: Auth, credentials, and account lifecycle form one ownership area.
  2. Catalog service: Course metadata, search-facing content, and publishing rules may belong together.
  3. Enrollment service: Enrollment lifecycle and seat allocation belong to one transactional core.
  4. Billing service: Payments and invoices often need stronger policy and compliance boundaries.

Why This Matters

The problem: Weak decomposition creates distributed monoliths where services are separate in deployment but still tightly coupled in behavior and data.

Before:

  • Splitting by technical layer or persistence shape.
  • Services constantly calling each other to complete one basic workflow.
  • Ownership disputes over data and behavior.

After:

  • Boundaries reflect business capabilities and policy ownership.
  • Data ownership follows service responsibility more clearly.
  • The system can evolve with less cross-service friction.

Real-world impact: Better decomposition reduces coupling, simplifies ownership, and makes microservice autonomy more than a slogan.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this session, you will be able to:

  1. Explain what makes a service boundary strong - Connect decomposition to capability, ownership, and change patterns.
  2. Reason about data ownership - Understand why service boundaries often imply ownership of the corresponding domain data.
  3. Spot decomposition mistakes - Recognize overly fine-grained or technically sliced service boundaries.

Core Concepts Explained

Concept 1: Boundaries Should Follow Business Capability

Concrete example / mini-scenario: Enrollment is more than writing rows into a table. It includes seat allocation, eligibility rules, waitlists, and enrollment history. Those behaviors belong together better than a table-based split would suggest.

What it is: Business-capability decomposition groups behavior around what the system does for the business or product, not around low-level implementation artifacts.

Why it matters: Capabilities change for business reasons, which is exactly the kind of change a service boundary should isolate.

How it works: Teams identify cohesive workflows, policies, and ownership areas, then align services to those boundaries.

Mental model: A service should own a chapter of the business story, not one paragraph from every chapter.

When to use it:

  • [Ideal situation] Domains with recognizable capabilities such as identity, billing, catalog, or fulfillment.
  • [Anti-pattern] Decomposing around controllers, tables, or utility code because those are easier to see.

Concept 2: Data Ownership Usually Follows Behavioral Ownership

Concrete example / mini-scenario: If the enrollment service owns eligibility and seat allocation, it should usually own the enrollment records and seat state too rather than sharing those writes freely with other services.

What it is: A strong service boundary usually includes authority over the data needed to enforce the policies and workflows inside that boundary.

Why it matters: Shared write ownership across services quickly recreates tight coupling and unclear authority.

How it works: Each service becomes authoritative for its core data, while other services integrate through contracts, replicated views, or events rather than direct shared mutation.

Mental model: If a service is responsible for the rule, it usually needs to own the record that proves and enforces that rule.

Code Example (If applicable):

service_ownership = {
    "identity": ["users", "credentials"],
    "catalog": ["courses", "course_publication_state"],
    "enrollment": ["enrollments", "seat_allocations"],
}

Note: The point is not literal table naming. The lesson is that ownership should align with business authority.

When to use it:

  • [Ideal situation] When the team wants to reduce ambiguity about which service controls critical state.
  • [Anti-pattern] Shared-write databases across "separate" services.

Concept 3: Over-Decomposition Creates Coordination Debt

Concrete example / mini-scenario: Splitting notifications into email-template service, delivery service, preferences service, audit service, and event-format service may sound precise, but it can also create many network calls just to send one message.

What it is: Over-decomposition happens when service boundaries become so fine-grained that the system spends more effort coordinating services than benefiting from their independence.

Why it matters: Very small services increase operational cost, latency, version management, and cognitive overhead.

How it works: Teams recognize when a proposed boundary lacks enough independent policy or change pressure to justify becoming its own service.

Mental model: A service should be small enough to own, but large enough to matter.

When to use it:

  • [Ideal situation] As a review question when service count keeps growing.
  • [Anti-pattern] Equating "micro" with "as many deployables as possible."

Troubleshooting

Issue: Splitting by data shape rather than business behavior.

Why it happens / is confusing: Tables and schemas are visible, so they can dominate the decomposition discussion.

Clarification / Fix: Start from workflows, policies, and ownership. Data design should reinforce the business boundary, not define it alone.

Issue: Mistaking small services for good boundaries.

Why it happens / is confusing: "Micro" sounds like a size target.

Clarification / Fix: Optimize for cohesive ownership and independent evolution, not minimum code size.

Advanced Connections

Connection 1: Decomposition ↔ Domain-Driven Design

The parallel: Bounded contexts provide one of the strongest mental models for deciding where service boundaries may belong.

Connection 2: Decomposition ↔ Data Consistency

The parallel: Once data ownership is split across services, consistency across the system becomes an integration problem rather than one local transaction.

Real-world case: Enrollment and billing may coordinate through events or sagas rather than one shared database transaction.

Key Insights

  1. Good service boundaries follow business capability - That is usually stronger than table- or layer-based decomposition.
  2. Data ownership should align with policy ownership - Shared write authority weakens service boundaries quickly.
  3. Over-decomposition creates coordination debt - More services are not automatically better architecture.

Knowledge Check (Test Questions)

  1. Why is "one service per table" usually a weak decomposition rule?

    • A) Because data shape alone rarely captures the full business capability and policy boundary.
    • B) Because services should never own data.
    • C) Because databases disappear in microservices.

  2. What usually follows a strong service boundary?

    • A) Clear authority over the behavior and data needed to enforce that capability.
    • B) Shared write access by every other service.
    • C) Zero need for integration with other services.

  3. Why can too many tiny services be harmful?

    • A) Because they increase coordination, latency, and operational overhead without enough independent benefit.
    • B) Because smaller services can never scale.
    • C) Because only monoliths can represent domains.

Answers

1. A: Table structure is not the same thing as business responsibility, which is what service boundaries should reflect.

2. A: Strong boundaries usually pair behavioral authority with ownership of the relevant state.

3. A: Excessively fine-grained services often create more distributed coordination than meaningful autonomy.
