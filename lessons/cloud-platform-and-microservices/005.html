Service-to-Service Network Policy

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to...

  • turn one remote dependency into an explicit call policy;

  • investigate a timeout without mistaking it for proof that no work happened; and

  • choose a mitigation that contains overload without silently changing a business outcome.

Idea in one sentence: A service call is reliable only when its deadline, retry safety, caller identity, evidence, and domain fallback describe what the system will do when the network cannot provide a clear answer.

Core Insight

The previous lesson chose an interaction shape for a paid course purchase. Enrollment needs an immediate billing answer before it can promise access; notifications and analytics can happen later. That design is only the beginning. The synchronous billing edge now needs a rule for the moment when the answer is late, lost, or ambiguous.

Consider a Monday launch. Learners press Enroll, and the enrollment service asks billing to authorize payment. The payment provider slows down. Enrollment returns errors after its client timeout, but some provider authorizations may still finish. A well-meaning client retries every failed request three times. Billing receives a new burst of work precisely when it is least able to handle it. Support can see a failed enrollment, but cannot tell whether a charge was made.

The tempting initial model is: a timeout means the operation failed, so retry until it succeeds. It is attractive because it turns uncertainty into a simple loop. It fails because a timeout describes what the caller did not hear, not what the callee did or did not do. A retry can amplify an overloaded dependency or repeat a side effect.

The stronger model is: each remote operation has a policy contract before it has a client default. That contract says how long the caller can wait, what makes a retry safe, who is calling, how the path is observed, and which visible domain state preserves correctness when the result remains uncertain. It works whether the mechanics later live in application code, a shared library, a proxy, or a service mesh. This lesson is about the contract, not a particular implementation.

The Symptom Is an Ambiguous Outcome

Start with the production symptom rather than a configuration knob:

Learner sees:       "We could not confirm your payment."
Enrollment record:  failed or missing
Billing trace:      client deadline exceeded
Provider record:    authorization may be pending or complete

The system has evidence of a failed conversation, not evidence of a failed payment. Several things may have happened:

What might have happened What the timeout tells enrollment Safe conclusion
Billing never received the request. No response arrived in time. The payment was probably not attempted, but investigate before assuming.
Billing received it but was slow. No response arrived in time. Work may continue after enrollment gives up.
Billing authorized payment, but its response was lost. No response arrived in time. A second authorization may create a duplicate attempt.
Billing rejected the request after the deadline. No response arrived in time. The caller needs the actual result, not a guessed transport outcome.

That uncertainty is normal at a network boundary. Local calls can throw a visible exception after a method fails. Remote calls cross queues, sockets, processes, and sometimes another provider. No client setting removes the possibility that the server completed work after the client stopped waiting.

For this reason, a useful incident question is not “Why did the request fail?” It is “Which outcome is still possible, and where is the authoritative evidence?” For payment authorization, billing owns the attempt record and the provider reference. Enrollment owns the learner-facing state. Both need enough identifiers and trace context to join the evidence without guessing from a timestamp.

Build a Call Policy Before the Incident

Write the policy for one operation, not for “HTTP calls” in general. The following values are illustrative; a real team derives them from the user promise, observed latency, and downstream constraints.

operation:        authorize payment for an enrollment
caller:           enrollment-service
callee:           billing-service
caller deadline:  1,500 ms within a 2,000 ms learner request budget
retry rule:       no blind retry; repeat only with a billing-recognized idempotency key
identity:         enrollment-service is authorized for the billing endpoint
evidence:         trace context, enrollment id, billing attempt id, safe correlation fields
fallback:         enrollment becomes pending_payment; learner is told when confirmation is incomplete
owner of truth:   billing for payment status; enrollment for access status

Every line answers a different failure question.

Deadline. A deadline is the latest time at which a caller can still use an answer. It is more useful than an isolated timeout because it can flow down a request path. A timeout is often a local waiting limit; a propagated deadline preserves the remaining budget across services. Neither should consume the entire learner request budget, because enrollment still needs time to record its decision and respond.

Retry rule. “Three retries” is not a semantic rule. A read can often be retried if stale data is acceptable. A command needs the callee to recognize the same logical request. An idempotency key such as enrollment-741:payment-authorization lets billing associate repeated delivery attempts with one business operation. It does not make every side effect harmless by magic: billing still has to store and apply that key correctly, and the provider integration still needs its own safe behavior.

Identity. Authentication answers who presented credentials. A call policy also needs authorization: is enrollment-service allowed to ask billing to authorize a payment, and is a batch job allowed to do the same? A uniform workload identity mechanism is a platform concern; the permission to execute this domain operation is not merely a transport default.

Evidence. Trace context connects the learner request, enrollment, billing, and provider span. A stable enrollment identifier and billing attempt identifier make it possible to inspect the authoritative records. Be deliberate with attributes: an identifier class may be useful, while a raw payment token or unnecessary personal data does not belong in broad telemetry.

Fallback. A fallback is not “return 200 anyway.” It is a product and domain decision. In this case, pending_payment preserves the truth: enrollment has not yet confirmed access, and billing may still determine the payment result. It gives the learner a visible state and gives operations a repair path.

Work the Request Budget End to End

The policy becomes concrete when a learner starts a paid enrollment. Assume a two-second user-facing budget. The numbers below teach the shape, not a universal latency target.

learner request: 2,000 ms total
  enrollment validation and state write: reserve 350 ms
  enrollment -> billing: use at most 1,200 ms of remaining deadline
    billing -> provider: use only its remaining budget, for example 750 ms
  enrollment response and pending-state write: reserve the rest

Now trace a slow provider response.

Step What happens What enrollment should do
1 Enrollment creates or finds one billing attempt using the enrollment id. Carry the deadline and trace context; do not invent a second business operation.
2 Billing calls the provider. The provider is slow. Stop waiting when its remaining budget expires; record enough state to reconcile later.
3 Enrollment reaches its own deadline before a result returns. Write pending_payment, return an honest pending message, and retain the correlation id.
4 Billing later receives a provider result. Update the payment attempt and notify or expose the authoritative status through the chosen workflow.
5 The learner retries from the UI. Reuse the logical enrollment/payment attempt rather than automatically authorizing a new payment.

The critical choice is not the exact millisecond values. It is the invariant that downstream work does not outlive a caller’s useful window without a named recovery path. If billing is allowed to keep reconciling a provider result after the user response, that is valid work—but it should be deliberate background work tied to the same attempt, not an accidental orphaned request.

This also explains why one blanket timeout policy fails. A low-latency eligibility read and a payment authorization have different correctness costs. The platform can provide sensible defaults, deadline propagation, connection limits, and retry budgets. The owning service must state whether a repeat is safe and what uncertainty means to the learner.

Investigate the Failure Chain

When the symptom appears, investigate in order of evidence rather than adjusting retries first.

  1. Identify the logical operation. Start with the enrollment id or idempotency key, not just a transient request id. Ask whether there is one attempt or several.
  2. Follow the trace. Did enrollment call billing? Did billing start a provider request? Where was time spent, and which deadline expired?
  3. Check authoritative state. Billing’s attempt record and provider reference determine whether payment is pending, authorized, declined, or unknown. Enrollment’s record determines whether access was granted.
  4. Measure the amplification. Compare request rate, retry count, concurrent calls, timeout rate, and downstream saturation before and during the incident. A rise in retries alongside latency is evidence that callers may be making recovery harder.
  5. Contain and repair. Reduce unsafe retries or shed nonessential work according to the policy. Reconcile pending attempts; do not bulk-retry payments merely because a dashboard shows timeouts.

Useful signals make this sequence fast:

Signal What it can reveal Caution
End-to-end trace duration Which hop used the available budget. A missing span is an observability gap, not proof a call was absent.
Deadline-exceeded rate by caller and operation Which contract is failing. Group by operation; fleet-wide averages hide a broken path.
Retry attempts and retry budget exhaustion Whether clients are amplifying load. A lower error rate can still hide duplicate work.
Billing attempts by outcome and age Whether pending work is being reconciled. Track unknown/pending separately from confirmed decline.
Connection saturation or queue depth Whether the dependency has a capacity problem. Do not treat the signal alone as a license for indefinite retries.

The principle is simple: transport metrics show stress; domain records establish outcome. Incident response needs both.

Put Each Responsibility at the Right Layer

Consistency is valuable, but centralization can erase meaning. Split the policy by the layer that can responsibly own it.

The trade-off is fleet consistency versus domain precision: shared runtime rules make incidents easier to contain, while the service owners must retain the decisions that determine whether a repeated or uncertain operation is correct.

Concern Likely owner Reason
TLS, workload identity, deadline propagation, standard trace context platform or shared runtime Every service benefits from consistent mechanics.
Connection pools, bounded concurrency, retry budget defaults platform with service configuration Fleet safeguards should be coherent, while calls vary.
Whether a request is idempotent domain-owning service Only the owner can define “the same logical command.”
Whether access is granted while payment is uncertain enrollment and billing workflow owners This changes the product promise and correctness model.
Reconciliation and operator repair path service owners, with platform visibility The owner knows the authoritative state; the platform supplies evidence and guardrails.

A circuit breaker, proxy, or later service mesh can help enforce bounded failure behavior. It cannot decide whether a delayed payment means “grant access,” “wait,” or “cancel.” Equally, application teams should not each invent their own identity and trace format. Good platform policy supplies reliable rails; domain policy supplies the semantic destination.

Common Confusions

Confusion: “A timeout proves the payment failed.”

Why it is tempting: the caller received an error.

Better model: it proves the caller did not receive an answer by its deadline. Inspect the authoritative attempt before creating another business operation.

Confusion: “Retries are a reliability feature, so more retries are safer.”

Why it is tempting: a transient network error sometimes succeeds on the next try.

Better model: retry only when the operation’s semantics and remaining deadline make it safe. Bound retries and use jitter where appropriate so clients do not synchronize new load onto a struggling dependency.

Confusion: “The platform can solve idempotency.”

Why it is tempting: a shared middleware layer can attach a key.

Better model: infrastructure can propagate a key, but billing must define how it maps to one payment attempt and how long that mapping remains valid.

Confusion: “Fallback means hiding the dependency failure.”

Why it is tempting: a successful HTTP response looks healthy on a dashboard.

Better model: a fallback is correct only when it communicates a truthful domain state. pending_payment can be safer than an invented success or an irreversible rejection.

Check Your Understanding

Check: Enrollment times out after 1.2 seconds while billing may still be waiting for the provider. A teammate proposes immediately sending a second authorization because the first response was missing. What is the first design question?

Think first, then reveal.

Answer: Ask whether billing can recognize both deliveries as the same logical payment attempt. If it cannot, the second request may duplicate a side effect. Preserve the attempt id, query or reconcile its authoritative state, and only repeat under a billing-owned idempotency rule.

Check: A platform team wants one retry setting for all services. What should they centralize, and what must remain local?

Think first, then reveal.

Answer: They can centralize guardrails such as bounded retry budgets, deadline propagation, telemetry, and safe default backoff. Each service must still classify its operation, idempotency semantics, and domain fallback. A uniform mechanism is useful; a uniform business consequence is not.

Practice

Review this edge in a course-purchase flow:

checkout -> enrollment: grant course access

Write a six-line policy: caller, callee, deadline, retry posture, evidence, and fallback. Then test it against this incident: checkout receives a timeout after enrollment may have already granted access.

A strong answer does not simply say “retry twice.” It explains whether grant course access has an idempotency key, how checkout discovers the authoritative enrollment state, and what the learner sees while it is uncertain. It also reserves part of the user-facing budget for a truthful response instead of forwarding the entire deadline blindly downstream.

Connections

The preceding lesson chose whether an interaction needs an immediate answer, delegates work, or announces a fact. This lesson gives the immediate edges—and any delivery attempt—a failure contract. The next lesson explains how a caller locates a healthy instance once it has a policy for the call.

Key Takeaways

  • A timeout reports missing timely evidence, not necessarily a missing side effect.
  • A call policy must join deadline, retry safety, identity, observability, and a truthful domain fallback.
  • Platform guardrails can standardize transport mechanics; services retain idempotency and business-outcome decisions.
  • Investigate with traces and authoritative domain records before retrying ambiguous commands.
