Service Decomposition and Boundary Design

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to...

  • test a proposed service boundary against language, authority, and workflow pressure;

  • distinguish a cohesive capability from a fragmented distributed workflow; and

  • choose a low-cost boundary test before extraction.

Idea in one sentence: A service boundary is strong when one capability can own its decisions and critical writes while related rules change together often enough to repay the coordination it creates.

Core Insight

Suppose a learning platform wants separate services for eligibility, seat allocation, waitlists, cancellation, and enrollment history. Each name sounds focused. But a learner’s attempt to take a course needs those rules to decide together.

The first model is: small services are good services. It works only when each small unit owns a real decision. Here, every ordinary enrollment would coordinate five remote pieces that share the same vocabulary and state.

The stronger model is to design around a capability, not a table, endpoint, or folder. Enrollment may own eligibility, seat allocation, waitlists, cancellation, and enrollment history because they define one promise: whether this learner gets a place. That capability can later become a service only if its authority and change pressure are real.

The Promise We Need to Keep

The course-purchase flow needs different owners without a hidden shared decision:

catalog:    is the course offered and what is its public information?
billing:    was payment authorized and what is its audit record?
enrollment: may this learner take a place, and what is their access state?
notification: how is the outcome communicated?

Enrollment needs billing’s result for a paid path, but it should not own the payment attempt. Billing may publish a payment fact, but it should not allocate a seat. This is a boundary of authority, not a promise that services never interact.

The Naive Design

Splitting enrollment into eligibility-service, seat-service, and waitlist-service looks granular. In the normal workflow, however, eligibility needs current seats, seat allocation may create a waitlist position, and cancellation may promote a learner. No service can explain the outcome alone.

learner requests a seat
  -> eligibility asks seat service
  -> seat service asks waitlist service
  -> each service writes part of one enrollment decision

The diagram has more boxes. The decision is still one policy area. A timeout or partial update now makes ordinary enrollment a distributed repair problem.

Design Alternatives

Boundary Benefit Cost or warning
One enrollment capability One owner can protect access and seat invariants. It may be a larger internal module.
Tiny services by table or endpoint Smaller deployables. Ordinary decisions cross many boundaries and share authority.
Billing separated from enrollment Different audit, provider, and operational pressure. Needs an explicit payment contract and uncertain-outcome policy.
Notifications separated Delivery has its own retries and provider operations. Enrollment must not depend on delivery for its access decision.

For this platform, keep enrollment together first. Treat billing and notification as candidate boundaries only because their language, authority, change pressure, and operating needs differ. This is a situated choice; a simple product may keep all three as well-designed modules for longer.

A Better Boundary Test

Test four agreements before drawing a remote API.

Agreement Question Enrollment evidence
Language Do the rules use one coherent vocabulary? eligibility, seat, waitlist, cancellation, access
Authority Who decides and protects critical state? enrollment is the only writer for seat and access state
Change Do these rules change together? a waitlist policy change affects allocation and cancellation
Operation Does the capability need distinct release, audit, or failure handling? payment audit points to billing; delayed delivery points to notifications

When the agreements align, a boundary can remove coordination. When they point in different directions, extraction freezes a guess into network calls.

Work the Purchase Flow

Trace one paid enrollment:

Step Owner Decision or fact
Course is selected catalog Public course is offered.
Payment is attempted billing Payment attempt is authorized, declined, or uncertain.
Seat is granted or held pending enrollment Learner gets access, waitlist status, or a pending state.
Receipt is sent notification Delivery is retried without changing access authority.

The useful question at each step is: “Who can explain this result and correct it?” If three services may edit the same enrollment row, the answer is unclear. If enrollment owns its records, it can consume a billing result without giving billing permission to allocate a seat.

So far, we have not chosen HTTP, events, or a database layout. We have established what must remain together and what can cross a contract. The next migration lesson proves whether this boundary can be operated independently.

Design Review Before Extraction

Use one product change to test the map. The platform introduces a rule: learners who cancel within two hours may rejoin a waitlist only if the course still has no free seat. This one change touches cancellation, seat allocation, and waitlist ordering.

Candidate design Who must change it? What the change reveals
One enrollment capability The enrollment owner changes one policy and its records. The rules are cohesive.
Separate cancellation, seat, and waitlist services Three owners must coordinate versions, calls, and partial failure. The proposed boundaries divide one decision.
Catalog owns the rule Catalog must now understand access and waitlist state. The rule crossed into the wrong authority.

This is the workflow pressure test. A static diagram can make all three designs look clean. A real policy change asks who must agree, which state they may change, and what happens if the second step fails after the first succeeds. If the ordinary answer is “several services must update their own pieces,” the system has likely exported one invariant into the network.

The preferred first move is not necessarily a new deployable. Keep the enrollment capability behind an internal interface, prohibit external writes to its seat and access records, and make the policy change there. Then watch a real change: did catalog or billing need to reach into enrollment internals? Did the team need a coordinated release? Can support explain a cancelled learner’s state from one owner? Those observations earn a later extraction decision better than a component diagram does.

This test also exposes a useful limit. A capability can be cohesive and still remain a module because the product is changing too quickly, the team is small, or the operational burden of a remote boundary would exceed its benefit. “Keep it together for now” is a design decision, not a failure to modernize.

The relevant signal is coordination during a meaningful, realistic policy change, not the number of boxes in an architecture diagram. It must show a durable owner.

Consequences, Trade-offs, and Limits

This design improves ownership and makes failure investigation more direct. It costs discipline: callers must stop bypassing the owner, and teams must create contracts for real cross-capability work.

The trade-off is granularity versus cohesion. More deployables can enable independent change, but every boundary adds latency, compatibility work, and partial failure. The idea does not decide an API protocol or solve distributed transactions. The warning signal is workflow friction: a basic enrollment needs many synchronous calls, shared writes, or multiple teams to explain one outcome.

Common Confusions

Confusion: “One service per table is clean.”

Why it is tempting: tables are visible technical units.

Better model: storage shape does not define policy ownership. Start from behavior, language, and the invariant that must remain explainable.

Confusion: “No shared database means a strong boundary.”

Why it is tempting: shared writes are dangerous.

Better model: protected writes are necessary but not sufficient. The capability still needs coherent language, authority, change pressure, and an operational reason.

Confusion: “Independent services never need to coordinate.”

Why it is tempting: autonomy sounds like isolation.

Better model: different capabilities interact through contracts. The goal is explicit coordination, not zero coordination.

Check Your Understanding

Check: Catalog, billing, and enrollment can all update the same seat count. Is enrollment a strong authority boundary?

Think first, then reveal.

Answer: No. Several writers can change the state that determines the access decision. Clarify the owner and route writes through it before treating the boundary as independent.

Practice

The team proposes a separate coupon-validation service. Catalog, billing, and enrollment all change discount rules together and all write the discount table.

A good review should mention the shared language and writes, the workflow that needs the rules, and one low-cost test: assign one owner, hide direct writes behind an internal interface, and observe whether other capabilities can use a stable contract. The current proposal is not a proven service boundary.

Key Takeaways

  • Design boundaries around cohesive decisions, not tables or component count.
  • One owner should protect the critical writes that enforce a capability’s policy.
  • Walk a real workflow: shared writes and chatty coordination reveal fragile decomposition.
  • A modular internal interface is a low-cost test before a network boundary becomes expensive.
