Disagreement as Information

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to...

  • Separate disagreement about a fact from disagreement about confidence or action.

  • Use a disagreement to inspect evidence, definitions, time ranges, and hidden assumptions.

  • Choose a proportionate response instead of dismissing the other person or splitting the difference automatically.

Idea in one sentence: Disagreement is useful information when the other person's view gives us a new reason to inspect our own evidence, but it does not make every view equally strong.

Core Insight

Imagine that your family is looking at an old photograph of the house where you grew up. You say:

The piano was in the living room.

Your sister says:

No. It was in the small den.

You both lived in the house. You both remember the room clearly. Neither of you is obviously lying, and neither of you is obviously better at remembering this kind of event.

This is not just an annoying argument. It gives you information about your own belief. If another person who was similarly placed reached a different answer, then at least one of you may have missed something, remembered a different period, or used the words “the piano” or “the living room” differently.

The disagreement does not tell you who is right by itself. It tells you that your current support may not be as decisive as it felt.

The Small Disagreement

Start with the exact claim:

Claim: The piano was in the living room in 1998.

Now describe the disagreement precisely.

Part Family example Question
Claim The piano was in the living room in 1998. What proposition are we comparing?
My view I believe the claim. What do I currently accept?
Sister's view She believes the piano was in the den. What alternative does she offer?
Shared position We both lived there and saw the rooms often. Are we similarly placed to judge this claim?
Evidence A photograph, floor plan, diary, or another memory. What could change either view?
Action Label the photograph or tell the family story. What depends on resolving the disagreement?

This is a disagreement about a belief: the two people assign different answers to the same factual question. It is different from an action disagreement such as “Should we move the piano now?” Two people can agree about where the piano is and still disagree about what to do.

Plain meaning, scenario, precise term

Plain meaning: Disagreement is a difference in belief, confidence, or conclusion about a question.

In this scenario: You and your sister disagree about the piano's location. Because you shared the house and have no obvious advantage, her memory is evidence that your own memory may need review.

Technical names: An epistemic peer is someone who is roughly as well placed as you are to get a particular question right. Peer is always relative to a question. Your sister may be your peer about the old house and not your peer about restoring a piano.

The Initial Model: One of Us Must Be Irrational

The first reaction is often:

My memory feels clear.
Therefore, my sister must be confused.

This model is attractive because confidence feels like evidence from the inside. It also protects identity: admitting a mistake can feel like losing a small piece of our history.

But the disagreement adds a fact about the reliability of our process. If two similarly placed people remember different rooms, then at least one memory is wrong, incomplete, or answering a slightly different question.

The better model is:

My evidence supports the living-room claim.
Another similarly placed person has conflicting evidence.
Therefore, I should inspect the disagreement before treating my confidence as decisive.

This does not require surrendering the belief. It requires treating the other person's disagreement as higher-order evidence: information about how reliable our current reasoning may be.

What Disagreement Can Reveal

A disagreement is not one single signal. Inspect what kind of difference is present.

1. Different claims

The two people may not be answering the same question.

You: The piano was in the living room in 1998.
Sister: The piano was in the den when our cousin visited.

Both statements could be true if the piano moved. The apparent disagreement reveals a missing time range.

2. Different definitions

Words can hide the disagreement.

You: The living room means the large room near the stairs.
Sister: The living room means the room where guests sat after the renovation.

Before comparing evidence, align the meaning of the key words.

3. Different evidence

One person may remember a scene while the other has a document or photograph. The disagreement tells you to compare the evidence, not just the confidence attached to it.

4. Different starting assumptions

People can see the same evidence but interpret it through different background beliefs. One person expects the piano to stay in one room; another remembers that the family moved it during repairs.

5. Different epistemic positions

One person may have an advantage relevant to the question. A person who was present during the move is better placed than someone who only saw a later photograph. Disagreement with a superior, a novice, or an unknown source should not be treated exactly like disagreement with a peer.

A Worked Disagreement Trace

Follow the family conversation step by step.

Moment New information What it changes
1 You remember the piano in the living room. Initial belief and memory-based support.
2 Your sister remembers it in the den. Higher-order evidence: a similar witness disagrees.
3 You compare dates and discover the piano was moved during renovation. The original claim was too broad.
4 A photograph from before the renovation shows the living room. Support for “living room before 1998 renovation.”
5 A diary entry from after the renovation says “practised in the den.” Support for “den after the renovation.”
6 You revise the family story. The disagreement exposed a missing time boundary.

The reasoning path is:

initial belief
    -> peer disagreement
    -> inspect claim, definitions, and dates
    -> compare independent evidence
    -> narrow the claim
    -> keep what the evidence supports

The final conclusion is not “we were both equally right about the original sentence.” It is:

The piano was in the living room before the renovation and in the den afterward. The disagreement came from using one sentence for two periods.

So far: Disagreement can reveal a false claim, a missing condition, a different definition, or a weakness in our evidence. Its value comes from the information it adds, not from the mere fact that two people spoke.

How Should You Respond?

There is no single response that fits every disagreement. Choose after checking the relationship between the people and the claim.

Conciliate

Lower your confidence when the other person is a competent peer and you have no clear reason to prefer your own evidence. The disagreement itself is a reason to look again.

Stay provisionally steady

Keep your view when you have a relevant advantage, such as a dated photograph you personally inspected, while still acknowledging that the other person may reveal a blind spot.

Suspend judgment

Pause when the evidence is balanced or the question cannot currently be resolved. “I do not know yet” is a real epistemic position, not a failure to choose a side.

Reject the disagreement as weak evidence

Do this only when the other view is clearly unrelated, uninformed, or based on a known defeater. A random guess and a carefully documented peer disagreement are not equal inputs.

These responses are compatible with respect. Changing confidence is not humiliation, and keeping a provisional belief is not automatically arrogance.

What Disagreement Does Not Mean

Disagreement does not imply that truth is only a matter of perspective. Two people can disagree and one can be wrong.

It also does not imply that the midpoint is correct. If one person says a bridge is safe and another says it is collapsing, “half safe” is not a useful conclusion. You need better evidence.

Finally, disagreement does not make every voice equally informative. A person may deserve equal respect while having less relevant evidence for this particular claim. Lesson 003 gave us the source-trust tools for making that distinction without turning credentials into immunity.

Trade-offs and Limits

The central trade-off is that taking disagreement seriously can protect us from overconfidence, but treating every disagreement as equally strong can produce confusion and false balance.

  • Dismiss every disagreement: fast and emotionally comfortable, but likely to hide mistakes.
  • Split the difference: feels fair, but can replace evidence with arithmetic.
  • Update after every disagreement: open-minded, but vulnerable to noise and manipulation.
  • Inspect the disagreement: slower, but lets us compare claims, evidence, positions, and stakes.

The method has limits. You may not know whether the other person is a peer. You may lack enough evidence to identify the better position. And some disagreements are deep: the people do not share the same definitions, standards, or background assumptions.

You can see the boundary when the disagreement changes the confidence you can reasonably have but does not tell you which conclusion is true. At that point, lower confidence, seek a better check, or suspend judgment rather than inventing a resolution.

Common Confusions

Confusion: Disagreement means both views are equally likely

Why it is tempting: equal treatment can sound like equal evidence.

Better model: Compare the people's positions, evidence, and domain fit. Respect is equal; evidential weight may not be.

Confusion: A confident person is a better epistemic peer

Why it is tempting: confidence is visible and persuasive.

Better model: Peerhood concerns how well placed someone is to get this question right, not how strongly they speak.

Confusion: The rational response is always to change your mind

Why it is tempting: stubbornness is a recognizable failure mode.

Better model: A relevant defeater should change confidence or trigger investigation. Weak disagreement may not require belief change.

Confusion: A compromise conclusion is always safer

Why it is tempting: compromise reduces social tension.

Better model: A narrower claim or a suspended judgment is often better than a midpoint that no evidence supports.

Check Your Understanding

Check: Two people remember a street name differently. One lived there for ten years; the other visited once. Is this automatically a peer disagreement?

Think first, then reveal.

Answer: No. The long-term resident may be better placed for this question, although memory can still be wrong. The disagreement should prompt a check, but the two positions do not have equal epistemic status automatically.

Check: A colleague says a meeting happened on Tuesday. You remember Wednesday, but you both have access to a calendar that says Tuesday. What should disagreement do here?

Think first, then reveal.

Answer: The shared calendar is stronger evidence than either memory. The disagreement should lead both people to update toward the dated record, unless there is a reason to doubt the calendar.

Practice: Review a Technical Disagreement

Two engineers inspect a slow service:

  • Engineer A says the database query is the cause.
  • Engineer B says the network between services is the cause.
  • A trace shows the query is fast when run directly, but the full request has a long gap before the database call.

Use the lesson's method:

  1. State the exact proposition they disagree about.
  2. Identify the evidence each view would need.
  3. Ask whether either engineer has a relevant advantage or whether they are peers on this diagnosis.
  4. Explain what the trace changes.
  5. Write the next check and a provisional conclusion.

A strong answer should say:

  • The disagreement is about the cause of the delay, not about whether the service is slow.
  • The trace weakens the “slow query” explanation and supports investigating the gap before the database call.
  • Neither title settles the question; the relevant evidence is timing and request flow.
  • A defensible conclusion is: “The query is not currently the leading explanation; inspect the pre-query network or queue path next.”

Key Takeaways

  • First check whether two people are answering the same question with the same definitions and time range.
  • Disagreement with a relevant peer is evidence that your own reasoning may contain an error or blind spot.
  • The rational response can be conciliation, provisional steadiness, investigation, or suspension; it is not always compromise.
  • Equal respect does not require equal evidential weight.
  • Use disagreement to find a better check, not to turn truth into a popularity contest.
